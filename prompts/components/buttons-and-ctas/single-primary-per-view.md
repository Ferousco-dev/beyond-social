A single view — a hero, a pricing card, an email — should ask the visitor to
do exactly one thing. Every additional CTA at the same visual weight splits
attention and measurably lowers the conversion rate of the one that mattered.

The recipe:

- Audit each screen or fold for its CTA count at full visual weight (solid
  fill, largest size): there should be one. Anything else demoted to
  secondary/tertiary treatment per the hierarchy pattern.
- When a page genuinely needs to serve two audiences (e.g. "Book a demo" for
  enterprise, "Start free trial" for self-serve), pick one as primary based on
  the page's stated business goal and demote the other to a text link or
  outline button — don't present them as equals and let the user's coin-flip
  decide.
- In email, resist the instinct to add a second CTA "just in case" — emails
  with a single, prominent CTA consistently outperform multi-CTA emails on
  click-through, because there's no attention to split.
- If a page must offer multiple paths (a comparison page, a multi-product
  hub), differentiate by hierarchy and grouping, not by giving each option
  its own full-weight button competing in the same visual band.
- Re-run this audit whenever a new banner, promo, or upsell gets added later
  by another team — CTA competition usually creeps in incrementally, not all
  at once.

Why: every additional full-weight CTA is a vote for a different next action,
and visitors resolve competing votes by picking the path of least
deliberation — often clicking neither, or clicking the wrong one for the
business. A/B tests removing a secondary CTA from a hero routinely lift the
primary CTA's click rate, because attention that used to split now converges.

Example: a SaaS hero with one solid "Start free trial" button and a plain
"Watch demo" text link below it, not a second full button beside it.

Counter-example: a hero with "Start free trial," "Book a demo," and "See
pricing" as three identical solid buttons in a row.
