Text overlays that appear and animate on top of short-form video, hook lines,
supporting claims, price flashes, need their own three-step scale (hook,
supporting, detail) with motion timing tied to the message's beat, not a
single reused animated-text template stretched across every overlay in the
clip.

- Hook text, the first 1 to 2 seconds that stops the scroll: largest size in
  the clip, roughly 10 to 12 percent of frame height, on screen just long
  enough to read once, 1.2 to 1.8 seconds, cut before it overstays.
- Supporting text, claims or steps during the body: mid-size, 6 to 8 percent
  of frame height, matched to the clip's caption scale so the two don't
  visually compete.
- Detail text, price or fine print: smallest, under 5 percent of frame
  height, on screen only as long as it takes to actually read, not shrunk
  further just to fit more.
- Vary entrance timing and easing slightly between overlays rather than
  repeating one uniform animation curve; a perfectly identical fade-and-scale
  on every overlay is a template signature, real edits have small per-cut
  timing variation because a human trimmed each one by eye.

Why: a single animated-text template applied uniformly regardless of message
weight flattens the pacing, every line gets equal visual emphasis, which both
weakens the hook, it no longer stands out from the fine print, and reads as
software-generated, since real editors size and time each overlay to match how
important that specific line is in that specific cut.

Example: hook "This changed everything" at 11 percent frame height for 1.4s,
supporting bullet text at 7 percent through the next three cuts, price flash
at 4.5 percent for 1s at the end, three distinct sizes, three distinct
timings.

Counter-example: every text overlay in the clip using the identical 8 percent
frame height and the identical 0.3s scale-and-fade-in animation, regardless of
whether it's the hook or a disclaimer, the unmistakable signature of a single
reused motion-graphics template.
