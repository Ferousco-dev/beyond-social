On-screen caption text in UGC-style ads has to carry brand tone while still
reading as something a real person typed, not brand copy — polished-brand
tone in the caption layer is one of the fastest ways to break the "this is
authentic" illusion the format depends on.

- Write captions the way a person texting would, not the way a brand
  headline would: lowercase starts are fine, contractions are default, and
  short fragments beat complete sentences ("actually obsessed" not "I am
  actually obsessed with this product").
- Keep the brand's real vocabulary constraints (claimed verbs, banned
  words) even inside the casual register — voice still holds, only the
  formality dial drops all the way down.
- Avoid caption clichés that read as templated UGC rather than real UGC:
  "wait for it," "the way I gasped," and similar phrases are so common in
  synthetic UGC ads that they now signal fake rather than authentic.
- Let captions be slightly imperfect — a missing period, an ampersand
  instead of "and," a trailing "..." — small deviations from house style
  read as more real than a perfectly formatted caption stack.
- Match caption energy to what's happening on screen at that exact frame,
  not to the overall ad's arc — captions that anticipate the payoff before
  the footage gets there read as scripted.

Why: the entire premise of UGC-style creative is that the audience reads it
as a peer's unscripted reaction, not brand messaging, so any caption that
reads as "written by marketing" collapses that premise and the ad loses the
trust advantage the format exists to capture in the first place.

Example: "not me buying this after one video 💀" over a product reveal frame
— fragment, lowercase, plausible as something a real person typed fast.

Counter-example: "Discover why thousands of customers love this
game-changing product!" as a caption over the same UGC-style footage — 
grammatically complete, brand-voiced copy that instantly signals the video
is an ad regardless of how authentic the footage itself looks.
