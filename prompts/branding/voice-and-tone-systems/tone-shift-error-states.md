Errors are the moment a brand's tone gets tested hardest: the user is already
frustrated, so any tone miscalibration reads as the brand being tone-deaf on
top of the product being broken.

- Strip humor and exclamation points entirely — a joke next to a failure reads
  as the brand not taking the user's problem seriously.
- State what happened in plain terms before what to do next: cause, then
  action. Don't open with an apology that delays the useful information.
- Use active, first-person-plural ownership for the product's own failures
  ("we couldn't save your changes") and neutral, non-blaming phrasing for user
  input errors ("that email format isn't valid") — never blame the user for a
  system fault, and never over-apologize for a simple validation catch.
- Keep sentence length short; a long, hedging error message reads as the
  brand stalling rather than fixing something.
- Never use "oops," "whoops," or "uh-oh" for anything involving lost data,
  failed payments, or account access — reserve casual failure language for
  genuinely trivial slips (a mistyped search with no consequence).

Why: tone in an error state has to carry two jobs at once — de-escalate the
user's frustration and rebuild confidence that the system is competent. Cute
or jokey language does neither; it signals the brand cares more about its
personality than about the user's problem, which is the opposite of what
trust-repair requires.

Example: "We couldn't save your last edit. Your work up to 2:41pm is safe —
try saving again." — plain, ownership-taking, gives a concrete safety fact.

Counter-example: "Yikes, that didn't work! 😅 Wanna give it another go?" for a
failed save — the levity undercuts the seriousness of potential data loss and
makes the user trust the system less, not more.
