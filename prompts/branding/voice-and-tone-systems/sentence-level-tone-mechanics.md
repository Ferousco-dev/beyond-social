Tone isn't set by word choice alone — it's carried at least as much by
sentence length, punctuation, and syntax, which is why two sentences using
identical vocabulary can read as completely different tones.

- Sentence length sets energy: short sentences (under 8 words) read as
  urgent or confident; longer, subordinate-clause sentences read as
  measured or cautious — match length to the tone dial, not just adjectives.
- Contractions ("we'll," "you're") lower formality reliably; their absence
  raises it — this is a more consistent lever than swapping vocabulary.
- Exclamation points raise energy fast and don't degrade gracefully — one
  per message maximum, and never in error or billing contexts regardless of
  how casual the brand voice is elsewhere.
- Sentence-initial "So," "Okay," or "Alright," signals conversational,
  lower-formality tone; starting with the subject noun signals a more formal,
  declarative register.
- Passive voice reads as evasive or institutional almost every time it
  appears in user-facing copy — active voice with a clear subject ("we
  couldn't save this") is the default lever for warmth and accountability,
  not just a grammar preference.

Why: writers often try to adjust tone by swapping in "friendlier" words while
leaving sentence structure untouched, which produces copy that still reads
stiff or still reads flippant regardless of the vocabulary — the structural
choices are doing more of the perceptual work than the words are, so a tone
system needs mechanical rules, not just a vocabulary list.

Example: raising warmth without raising formality: "We couldn't save that.
Let's try again." — short, active, one soft contraction, no punctuation
inflation.

Counter-example: "It is regrettable to inform you that an error has
occurred during the save operation." — friendlier vocabulary wouldn't fix
this; the passive voice and clause structure are what make it read cold no
matter which words fill the slots.
