An arc can compress time either through cutting or through one continuous, unbroken take, but these are opposite techniques that put the pacing labor on different elements, and mixing a half-committed version of both usually weakens whichever one was doing the work.

- If choosing a single take: the camera itself must carry the pacing, a push-in that accelerates fractionally into the turn, a hold that settles at resolution. Motion has to be motivated by the performance, not decorative.
- If choosing a cut-driven montage: let the editing do the pacing (see the shot-duration rhythm technique) and keep the camera within each shot comparatively simple, since the cuts are already carrying the emotional load.
- Decide which approach carries the arc before writing the shot list or the generation prompt; do not describe a single take with editing-style beats spliced in, or a montage with an unmoving, static camera throughout.
- A single take demands more from performance timing since there's no cut to hide a weak beat behind.

Why: a continuous take asks the audience to trust that nothing was hidden by a cut, so its camera movement has to visibly track the character's internal shift in real time, while a cut-driven sequence can distribute the emotional labor across several shorter, easier-to-control shots. Neither approach is inherently better; picking one and executing it fully reads as intentional, while a half-hearted blend of both usually reads as indecisive.

Example: "one continuous slow push-in from wide to close over 20 seconds, the rate of the push subtly increasing at the turn."
Counter-example: a single unbroken take with a locked-off, unmoving camera and no movement at the turn, so nothing in the shot carries the emotional shift despite the technical difficulty of holding the take.
