A short video only has room for three or four beats, so each one has to
increase either the stakes or the specificity of the last; a flat sequence of
equally-weighted facts reads as a list, not a story, and lists get abandoned
mid-scroll.

- Order beats by ascending intensity: smallest claim or lowest stakes first,
  largest or riskiest last, never the reverse.
- Make the escalation concrete, not adjectival: go from "it broke" to "it
  broke in nine seconds" to "it broke in nine seconds and voided the
  warranty," each beat adding a fact the last one didn't have.
- Cut any beat that repeats the intensity of the one before it; if two beats
  could swap order with no loss, one of them is dead weight.
- Keep the final pre-payoff beat at the highest tension point, then resolve
  it, don't add a beat after the peak that isn't the resolution itself.

Why: escalation is what makes "average watch time" curve upward instead of
decaying in a straight line, each beat has to earn the next few seconds of
attention by being a step up from the last, not a lateral move.

Example: beat 1, "it looked fine out of the box"; beat 2, "it failed the
first stress test"; beat 3, "it failed in a way the manufacturer said was
impossible"; payoff shows the actual failure on camera.
Counter-example: three beats that each independently describe a different
feature at the same level of interest, with no beat building on the one
before it; nothing in the middle raises the question the payoff needs to answer.
