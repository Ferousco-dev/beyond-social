A payoff resolved with a verbal claim ("and it works great") is weaker than a
payoff resolved with a single measurable, visible piece of evidence, because
the claim asks for trust while the evidence removes the need for it.

- Replace the summary sentence with an on-camera demonstration of the exact
  outcome the hook promised: the object surviving the drop, the timer
  reading the actual number, the before/after in the same frame.
- Pick a payoff that's falsifiable on camera, something a skeptical viewer
  could in principle check, rather than an assertion only the narrator can
  vouch for.
- Hold the proof shot long enough to register, roughly a full second of
  clean, uncut visual before cutting away, so it isn't skimmed past.
- If the real result is genuinely unglamorous, show that plainly rather than
  dressing it up with a claim; a modest but real result on camera outperforms
  an inflated verbal one.

Why: viewers on social platforms are calibrated to distrust marketing
language by default; a claim triggers skepticism while a specific, checkable
visual bypasses it, because the burden of proof has already been met on
screen instead of asserted.

Example: hook claims the coating is waterproof; payoff is a static shot of
water beading and rolling off the surface in real time, no voiceover needed.
Counter-example: hook makes the same claim, payoff is a smiling narrator
saying "and yeah, it's completely waterproof," with no demonstration on
screen; the exact thing the hook promised to show never appears.
