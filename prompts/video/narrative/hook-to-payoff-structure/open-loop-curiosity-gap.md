A short video keeps a viewer only as long as there's an unresolved question
on screen; naming that question in the first line, out loud or in text, gives
the whole runtime a reason to be watched to the end.

- State the gap as a concrete question or incomplete sentence, not a topic:
  "the part everyone gets wrong" beats "how this works."
- Keep exactly one open loop per short-form video; a second question
  introduced mid-video competes with the first and dilutes both.
- Withhold the answer's specifics, not its existence, don't hide that a
  reveal is coming, hide what the reveal actually is.
- Reference the loop again at least once before the payoff ("okay, here's
  where it went wrong") so viewers who drifted get pulled back in.

Why: curiosity is a debt the brain wants closed; an explicit, singular
question creates a stronger completion drive than an implicit one the viewer
has to infer, and one loop is resolvable in fifteen to sixty seconds while two
loops usually means neither gets a satisfying close.

Example: "I tried the $3 version against the $300 version, one of them
broke in under a minute, guess which" as the opening line, cut to product
shots, no answer until the final beat.
Counter-example: opening with "here are five things about X" then
resolving five separate mini-questions with no single throughline, so no
individual loop feels worth staying for.
