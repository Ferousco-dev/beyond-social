A closing shot that echoes the framing of the opening shot, same angle, same
composition, same subject position, but with the situation changed, reads as
resolved even without a spoken conclusion; it gives the ending a shape the
viewer can feel.

- Reuse the opening shot's camera position, lens, and framing for the
  closing shot, changing only what the story changed (the object, the
  subject's expression, the state of the thing being repaired or built).
- Keep the reused framing recognizable but not identical, a slight
  difference (light now on, mess now cleared, object now whole) is what
  registers as payoff rather than a repeat.
- Avoid explaining the parallel in dialogue or text; let the visual match do
  the work, naming it undercuts the effect.
- Reserve this for stories with a clear before/after state; it fails on
  videos where nothing physically or visibly changed.

Why: viewers register narrative closure through pattern completion as much
as through plot logic, a visual rhyme between first and last frame gives the
brain a concrete "this is finished" signal that a verbal wrap-up alone
doesn't supply as efficiently.

Example: open on a static handheld shot of a cluttered, dark workbench;
close on the same framing, same slight handheld drift, workbench now clear
with the finished piece sitting where the mess was.
Counter-example: opening and closing shots share no compositional
relationship at all, and the narrator states "and that's how we fixed it" to
compensate; the line does work the image should have done.
