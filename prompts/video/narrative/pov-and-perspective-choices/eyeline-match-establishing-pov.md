An eyeline match, cutting from a character looking off-frame to what they
are looking at, is the primary tool for telling a viewer "you are now seeing
what they see" without a slate, caption, or narration.

- Match screen direction between the look and the reveal: if the character
  looks left, the following shot places the subject left-of-center, not
  centered or reversed.
- Hold the look for a beat before cutting so the intent registers as a
  deliberate glance, not a stray movement.
- Set the reveal shot's height and angle to what that character could
  plausibly see from their actual eye level, not a generic camera height.
- Never use an eyeline match to reveal something the character has no
  physical line of sight to; it silently reassigns the POV to nobody.

Why: viewers already know this grammar from a lifetime of edited video;
breaking its direction or height rules does not just look sloppy, it
actively misassigns whose viewpoint the following shot belongs to and
confuses who is meant to be seeing what.

Example: character glances left, cut to the product sitting left-of-frame
on a shelf at roughly the character's own eye height.
Counter-example: character glances left, cut to an object framed
dead-center from a high overhead angle; the direction and height both contradict the look that set it up.
