A point of view sustained across multiple cuts only reads as one continuous
viewpoint if the implied lens, the handheld shake profile, and the exposure
response stay consistent from shot to shot.

- Keep focal length and field of view consistent for a given POV; a
  phone-in-hand POV should not intercut with a wide cinema-lens look for
  the "same" viewpoint.
- Match handheld shake amplitude and frequency across cuts; don't jump from
  a subtle drift in one shot to a shaky-cam spike in the next.
- Let exposure and white balance shift plausibly when the implied eyes move
  between lighting conditions, a brief half-second adjustment walking from
  shade into sun, rather than staying uniformly graded throughout.
- Keep any implied device artifacts (grain, rolling-shutter skew) the same
  across the sequence if the POV is meant to be one continuous device.

Why: viewers don't consciously track focal length or shake frequency, but a
discontinuity in either is read instantly as "this is a different camera,"
which quietly breaks the single-POV illusion even when the story content
stays continuous.

Example: three cuts of the same first-person POV, each with matching
handheld drift amplitude and a consistent slightly-wide field of view.
Counter-example: the first cut has a gentle handheld drift and the next cut
of the "same" POV has an aggressive whip-pan shake with a much narrower field of view; it reads as two different people's cameras.
