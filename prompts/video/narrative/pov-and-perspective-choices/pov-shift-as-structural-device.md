Deliberately switching whose eyes the camera represents partway through a
short video can serve as the structural turn itself, revealing information
the first POV had no access to.

- Signal the switch unambiguously: pair the cut with a change in framing
  convention (handheld first-person to a locked-off third-person shot), not
  just a change in subject.
- Use at most one POV shift in a sub-60-second video; a second shift reads
  as disorganized rather than intentional.
- Make the second POV answer a question the first POV's limits created,
  what the other person saw, knew, or was doing off-screen.
- Place the shift at the beat where the new information actually changes
  the viewer's understanding, not earlier or later for convenience.

Why: a POV shift is a strong reveal mechanism because it withholds
information structurally rather than just narratively, but it only reads as
a deliberate choice, instead of a continuity mistake, when the visual
convention change makes the switch impossible to miss.

Example: first-person POV of someone waiting nervously, hard cut to a
locked-off wide shot revealing what they couldn't see was already behind them.
Counter-example: the same footage but the second shot is handheld at a
similar height and framing to the first; the "reveal" reads as a jump cut, not a POV switch.
