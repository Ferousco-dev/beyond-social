Every ascent or descent should track a reason a person could name in one sentence.
Altitude change with no stated reason reads as the drone operator playing with the
controls rather than the story moving forward.

- Tie each rise or descent to a specific beat: rising to reveal scope, descending to
  introduce a person or product at eye level, holding steady while the subject itself
  changes.
- Match the rate of altitude change to the emotional pace of the moment: slow,
  even climbs for calm or grandeur, quicker climbs or drops for urgency.
- Never change altitude purely to "keep the shot interesting." If there's no
  answer to "why is the camera moving up right now," don't move it.
- Land the shot at a deliberate final altitude that matches whatever the next cut
  needs, not wherever the move happened to run out of runway.

Why: altitude is one of the few camera parameters an aerial shot can change that a
ground shot can't, which makes it powerful, but also makes it the first thing a
viewer notices being used without purpose. A climb that starts and stops for no
reason reads as pilot indecision; a climb tied to a beat reads as directed camera
work.

Example: "hold level at 15 feet through the interview setup, then climb steadily to
80 feet exactly as the narrator says 'and that was just the first location.'"

Counter-example: a drone that rises, drifts back down, and rises again over a static
shot with no corresponding change in the subject or the voiceover. The altitude
change registers as restlessness, not intent.
