Cutting between an aerial shot and a ground-level shot only preserves the viewer's
sense of place when the two shots share a legible anchor point and a consistent
direction of travel. Aerial footage exists outside the eyeline and geography rules
ground footage normally relies on, so continuity has to be built deliberately.

- Identify one object visible in both shots, a specific tree, doorway, or vehicle,
  that the viewer can use to relocate themselves after the cut.
- Keep the aerial camera's direction of travel consistent with the ground shot's
  screen direction; if the drone moved frame-left to frame-right, the ground shot
  should continue that vector rather than reverse it.
- Land the aerial shot's final framing close to the ground shot's opening framing in
  subject position, so the cut reads as a continuation rather than a scene change.
- When the geography genuinely changes to a new location, signal that deliberately
  (a wipe, a title card, a clear new landmark) rather than implying continuity a
  matched cut would otherwise suggest.

Why: at ground level, the 180-degree rule and consistent eyelines let an editor cut
between angles without disorienting the audience because both shots share a visible
reference frame. An aerial shot has no such shared frame by default, altitude and
angle change the entire visible world, so the editor has to manufacture the anchor
the cut needs, or the audience loses track of where they are the moment the shot
changes.

Example: "aerial arrives over the trailhead sign moving left to right, cut to a low
handheld shot starting on the same sign as the hiker walks past it left to right."

Counter-example: an aerial establishing a location while moving one direction, cut to
a ground shot moving the opposite direction with no shared object between them. The
viewer loses track of where they are relative to what they just saw.
