Aerial shots depend on light far more than ground shots do, because the sun's angle
is often the only thing modeling large-scale terrain. Flat overhead light removes
that modeling entirely and flattens the shot into a map.

- Shoot during golden hour or other low-sun hours so raking light throws long shadows
  that define hills, rooflines, and wave texture.
- Avoid midday sun straight overhead for aerials specifically; it casts almost no
  shadow at scale and turns varied terrain into a texture-less, shadowless surface.
- Backlight or sidelight water and grass to pick up specular sparkle and blade
  texture that flat frontlight won't reveal.
- Let distant layers desaturate and shift slightly toward blue relative to near
  layers. That atmospheric falloff is a genuine depth cue at altitude and shouldn't
  be corrected away in the name of clarity.

Why: at ground level, a subject's own three-dimensionality reads through occlusion
and perspective regardless of light angle. At altitude, terrain is mostly a
relatively flat plane being viewed from above, so shadow length and direction become
the primary way the camera reveals that a hillside has contour instead of being a
painted texture on a table.

Example: "shoot thirty minutes before sunset, sun raking from camera-left across the
ridgeline, long shadows defining every furrow in the plowed field."

Counter-example: "noon, sun directly overhead, no shadow direction specified." No
matter how high the resolution, the terrain reads as flat because there's nothing
in the light to describe its shape.
