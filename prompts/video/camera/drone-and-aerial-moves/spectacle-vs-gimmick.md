The same drone rise or orbit reads as spectacle when it is the one move doing real
work for a specific beat, and reads as gimmick when it is decoration layered onto a
shot that never needed it. The technique itself is neutral; placement decides which
one you get.

- Ask what information the aerial move delivers that a static or ground shot could
  not: scale, geography, a before/after relationship. If the answer is "nothing
  specific," it's decoration.
- Use aerial once per sequence, for the moment that earns it (the reveal, the "this is
  the whole operation" beat), not as connective tissue between every scene.
- Keep aerial shots shorter than the ground shots around them, roughly 2-4 seconds,
  rather than lingering on the view.
- Cut away at the shot's most information-dense frame, not after the momentum has
  already died.

Why: a viewer's tolerance for "impressive but empty" footage is low and drops fast
with repetition. The first drone rise in a video reads as scale; the fourth reads as a
filler transition, and it dilutes the first one retroactively by training the viewer
that aerial shots don't carry meaning in this particular video.

Example: a single drone rise used once, at the exact moment the full production floor
is revealed, sandwiched between two handheld interview shots.

Counter-example: opening every scene in the video with a drone rise "for production
value." The technique becomes wallpaper, and the one reveal that actually mattered
loses its impact because the audience has stopped expecting aerial shots to mean
anything.
