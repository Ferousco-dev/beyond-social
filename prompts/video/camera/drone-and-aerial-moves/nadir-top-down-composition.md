Straight-down (nadir) aerial shots work as graphic, pattern-based compositions, not
as narrative establishing shots, and should be framed like a print layout, not like a
documentarian revealing a place.

- Look for repeating or contrasting patterns visible directly overhead: rows of
  crops, a parking lot's grid, a pool's tile pattern, a market's stall layout.
- Frame for negative space and symmetry the way a poster or print layout would; a
  nadir shot reads as a composition first and a location second.
- Keep the drone genuinely level. This is the one aerial shot type where mechanical
  precision reads as intentional rather than synthetic, because the graphic read
  depends on true verticals and a locked horizon line.
- Use nadir shots for transitions, title cards, or a single graphic beat, not for
  establishing scale or geography; straight-down framing removes essentially all
  depth cues.

Why: without any horizon, shadow direction, or parallax between depth layers, a nadir
shot stops functioning like a camera move and starts functioning like a still
photograph or a printed pattern. Fighting that by adding a slow push or tilt usually
just weakens the graphic quality without adding back the depth information a normal
angle would have provided for free.

Example: "straight-down shot over the harvest, workers in colored shirts forming a
loose grid pattern against the tan field."

Counter-example: using a nadir shot as the video's establishing shot for a location.
It communicates shape and pattern, but tells the viewer nothing about scale, terrain,
or where they actually are.
