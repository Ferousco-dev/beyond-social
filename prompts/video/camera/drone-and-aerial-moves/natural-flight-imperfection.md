Real drone footage is never perfectly stable. Deliberately including the small,
specific imperfections a gimbal and inertial stabilizer leave in is what separates
footage that reads as "shot on a drone" from footage that reads as computer-rendered.

- A slight yaw drift of a degree or two over a long move, rather than a laser-locked
  heading held for the entire shot.
- A micro-altitude bob from wind, more pronounced near buildings or over open water
  where gusts are stronger and less predictable.
- A hint of prop-wash ripple on water or tall grass directly beneath the craft on low
  passes, a visible trace that something physical is disturbing the air.
- A brief exposure settle, a slight brightness shift in the first half-second after a
  big reveal into open sky, the way a real sensor's auto-exposure catches up.

Why: viewers register mechanical perfection as synthetic even when they can't
articulate why. Real gimbal-stabilized drone footage still carries small residual
motion and exposure behavior because it's compensating for a physical object being
pushed around by wind in real time; a frame that never wavers and never adjusts
exposure reads as an object with no physics acting on it at all.

Example: "camera holds a faint one-degree yaw wander through the rise, brightness
settles slightly as the wide sky enters frame at the top of the move."

Counter-example: "pixel-locked heading and exposure held constant for the entire
shot." Technically the cleanest possible result, but it registers as rendered rather
than captured, because nothing about it behaves like it was flying through moving air.
