An orbit (POI, point-of-interest) shot holds together only when the radius and
subject framing stay consistent through the arc. The instant either wobbles, it reads
as a mistake rather than a deliberate move; the instant either is too perfect, it
reads as a rendered turntable.

- Pick one fixed point on the subject (a rooftop peak, the center of a car's roof) as
  the orbit center, and hold camera-to-subject distance constant around it.
- Keep the subject at a consistent frame position through the whole arc (for example,
  right-of-center), not drifting toward an edge.
- Limit a single continuous orbit to roughly 90-160 degrees of travel. A full 360
  reads as a product demo or real-estate turntable, not a narrative shot.
- Let altitude drift by a few feet across the arc rather than staying perfectly
  level; a dead-flat orbit is one of the fastest tells of a locked, simulated path.

Why: an orbit is a promise to the viewer that they're circling a specific object in
real space. Radius or framing drift breaks that promise and reads as pilot error;
mechanical perfection breaks it the other way, reading as a 3D camera rig rather than
a craft flying in wind.

Example: "orbit right, 120 degrees, holding the lighthouse at the right third of
frame, altitude drifting up a few feet over the course of the arc."

Counter-example: "full 360-degree orbit at a perfectly locked altitude and radius."
It reads as a product-catalog turntable rather than a shot inside a story.
