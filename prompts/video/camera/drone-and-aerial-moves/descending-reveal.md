The inverse of rise-and-reveal, opening wide and high, then descending to introduce a
person or product at human scale, is the better choice when the story's payoff is
intimacy rather than scope. It collapses "the whole world" down to "this one detail."

- Open on the wide establishing frame first: the whole vineyard, the whole coastline,
  the whole factory floor.
- Descend continuously toward one specific point, slowing as the specific subject
  becomes identifiable rather than dropping at a constant rate throughout.
- Finish the descent at a height that reads as a person's eyeline or a tabletop
  height, not still visibly airborne.
- Hand the shot off to a ground camera at or near the landing point, matching
  framing or cutting to handheld, rather than cutting away from full altitude.

Why: rise-and-reveal earns its payoff by expanding the frame of reference; descending
reveal earns its payoff by narrowing it, which is the correct shape for origin
stories, craftsmanship, and product-in-context beats where the point is that this one
specific thing exists inside something much larger. Stopping the descent mid-air, at
neither the wide establishing height nor a human one, delivers neither payoff.

Example: "start above the whole coffee farm at sunrise, descend continuously toward
the drying patio, ending at eye level on the farmer's hands sorting beans."

Counter-example: "descend fast and stop mid-air at a generic middle altitude." The
shot never fully establishes scope and never arrives anywhere specific enough to feel
intimate; it just gets smaller.
