Aerial shots read as real captured space, rather than a flat rendered plate, when at
least three depth layers move against each other at visibly different apparent
speeds as the camera travels.

- Compose with near, mid, and far elements in frame together: foreground treetops or
  rooflines close to the lens, a midground of buildings or fields, a far horizon.
- Fly a path that isn't perfectly perpendicular to every layer, a slight diagonal
  drift relative to the scene keeps parallax visible rather than collapsing it.
- Favor lateral movement over a straight push-in when parallax is the goal; a
  push-in compresses depth cues because everything grows at a similar rate.
- Avoid dead-flat terrain with nothing near the camera. That is exactly the
  condition under which parallax disappears and the shot starts to look like a flat
  textured plane rather than real geography.

Why: at aerial altitude, most of the depth cues that work at ground level, focus
falloff, relative size of familiar objects, atmospheric perspective at close range,
weaken or vanish. Parallax (near things sliding past faster than far things) becomes
the dominant, sometimes the only, signal telling the viewer they're looking at
three-dimensional space rather than a photograph pasted onto a plane.

Example: "lateral pass along the coastline, foreground rocks sliding quickly through
frame, the town rooftops drifting more slowly behind them, the headland barely moving
against the horizon."

Counter-example: "aerial push straight down the center of a flat, empty field with no
foreground." Nothing in frame moves relative to anything else, and the shot reads as
a still image with a slow zoom rather than a camera in motion through real space.
