A fast, low-altitude flythrough works as a drone-dolly move, replacing a track or
gimbal, only when it threads through real obstacles rather than floating over open
space. Emptiness at low altitude just looks like a slow shot filmed too close to the
ground.

- Keep altitude within roughly a body length or two of the ground, water, or subject
  for the entire shot, not just at the start.
- Route the path through or beside real obstacles (dock pilings, a row of vines,
  market stalls) so parallax at multiple depths stays visible throughout.
- Match speed to obstacle density: slow through tight gaps, faster through open
  stretches, the way a human pilot actually flies rather than holding one constant
  speed regardless of what's around.
- Let altitude undulate slightly with the terrain contour instead of holding a
  perfectly flat line.

Why: at low altitude, the ground plane fills much of the frame, so any lack of
texture or obstacle reads immediately as empty, dead space. A pilot naturally slows
for tight gaps and speeds up in the open; a flat, constant-speed pass over nothing in
particular is the signature of a flight path plotted on a grid rather than flown.

Example: "skim six feet above the vineyard row, weaving slightly around a support
post, terrain rising gently with the hill."

Counter-example: "perfectly flat, perfectly centered flight straight down an empty
road at constant speed." No parallax variation, no obstacle negotiation, reads like a
flight-sim recording rather than captured footage.
