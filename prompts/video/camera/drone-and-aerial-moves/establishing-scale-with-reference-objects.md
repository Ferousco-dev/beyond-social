Aerial footage reads as spectacle only when the frame contains something the viewer
already knows the size of. Without that anchor, altitude just produces an abstract
picture with no sense of how big anything is.

- Put one human-scale reference in the frame at some point in the shot: a car, a
  person, a doorway, a boat, a parked truck.
- Avoid pure landscape aerials with nothing else in frame; they read as stock texture
  or wallpaper, not as a specific place with a specific size.
- When the subject itself is large (a ship, a stadium, a warehouse), pass a
  known-size object early in the shot before pulling back to the full wide.
- Set altitude to match what needs to stay legible. Fly too high and the subject
  shrinks past the point of registering as anything specific.

Why: the brain estimates size relationally, not absolutely. A silo photographed alone
against open sky could be ten feet or a hundred; the same silo next to a pickup truck
is unambiguous. Aerial shots lose the ground-level cues (people's height, doorframes)
that anchor scale automatically, so the shot has to reintroduce one on purpose.

Example: "aerial approach over the marina, gulls and moored sailboats passing beneath
the lens, then rise to reveal the full harbor with the client's boat visible near
center frame."

Counter-example: "wide aerial of open ocean, no vessels or landmarks in frame." It is
technically drone footage but conveys no scale, no location, and no story.
