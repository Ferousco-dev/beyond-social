A camera move should have a job a viewer can name without being told: it
follows an action, reveals new information, or tracks a shift in the
subject's state. Unmotivated movement is one of the fastest tells of
synthetic video.

- Before choosing any move, name its job in one clause: "reveals the room,"
  "tracks her decision," "raises the stakes." If no clause fits, hold static
  instead.
- Match move speed to the pace of what's happening in frame: a slow line of
  dialogue gets a slow push, a scramble gets a fast whip or handheld follow.
- End every move on a frame that could stand alone as a still; an arbitrary
  final composition means the move never had a destination.
- Prefer holding until an action starts the move, rather than starting the
  move and hoping an action catches up to it.

Why: human camera operators move for a reason, chasing action, reacting to
something, finding a better angle, even in the most polished productions. A
camera that drifts, orbits, or cranes with no in-world cause is the single
strongest visual signature of generated video, because generative models
default to smooth, decorative motion with no relationship to story.

Example: "static wide shot holds until she stands up to answer the door,
then the camera dollies with her toward the entrance."

Counter-example: a slow, ambient crane move across an empty room where
nothing happens — technically smooth, but with no action or reveal driving
it, it reads instantly as generated b-roll rather than footage of something
real occurring.
