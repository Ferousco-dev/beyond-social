A crab or truck move translates the camera sideways, parallel to a moving
subject or along a static row of subjects, producing parallax that a pan
executed in place cannot.

- Keep a constant distance and frame size to the subject or row throughout
  the move; any drift in scale reads as the rig losing its line.
- Keep the lens perpendicular to a product row so each item gets equal
  screen time and equal apparent size.
- For a moving subject, hold them in the same third of frame the entire
  move; leading or trailing them reads as the camera struggling to keep up.
- Set the travel speed so each item of interest gets roughly one full second
  of clear, unobstructed frame time.

Why: lateral tracking reveals depth through differential parallax, near
objects sweeping past faster than far ones, which is the specific visual cue
that separates "a row of things passed through" from "a flat photo of a row
of things." A pan in place has no such separation because the camera itself
never changes position.

Example: "camera trucks laterally past four bottles lined on a counter, each
held in frame for one second, consistent distance and eye-level height
throughout."

Counter-example: panning in place across the same four bottles — no parallax
separation, so the shelf reads flat and the move adds nothing a still photo
couldn't already show.
