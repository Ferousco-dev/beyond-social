A dolly zoom moves the camera one direction while zooming the opposite
direction so the subject's frame size stays constant while the background
visibly stretches or compresses, the classic effect popularized by
Hitchcock's Vertigo.

- Lock the subject's frame size throughout; any drift in their size at all
  breaks the illusion and it just reads as a bad zoom.
- Use the slow variant, 2-4 seconds, for creeping dread; use the sub-1-second
  fast variant for a shock or gut-punch beat.
- Reserve it for exactly one moment in a piece: a realization, a reveal of
  danger, never as a passive transition between beats.
- Never combine it with a simultaneous subject action (turning, standing) —
  the effect needs a still subject to read clearly.

Why: the effect isolates the one variable a normal stable frame hides, the
actual physical distance between camera and background, so the world visibly
stretches while the subject appears frozen. Nothing else in the visual
vocabulary produces that specific disagreement between subject and
background, which is why it reads as wrongness rather than a camera trick.

Example: "slow dolly-zoom on his face as he realizes the phone is dead,
background bookshelf stretching away over 3 seconds while his framing stays
locked."

Counter-example: applying a dolly zoom to a casual product shot — the effect
carries so much dread and vertigo connotation that using it outside a
realization beat reads as a mistake, not a stylistic choice.
