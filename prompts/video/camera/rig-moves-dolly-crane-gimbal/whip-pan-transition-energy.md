A whip pan blurs the frame in a fast horizontal sweep and is used to cut two
shots together on the blur, so a hard cut disappears inside apparent
continuous motion.

- Keep the actual blur short: 3-6 frames of unreadable motion; longer than
  that reads as a slow pan, not a whip.
- End shot A whipping out of frame, start shot B whipping into frame, and cut
  on the overlapping blur so the two whips read as one gesture.
- Motivate the direction with something in-frame: a person exiting, a head
  turn toward an off-screen sound, not an arbitrary sweep.
- Reserve it for a genuine tempo shift in the edit; using it between every
  cut flattens its impact into a tic.

Why: the eye cannot resolve heavily motion-blurred frames, so the cut hides
inside the blur instead of announcing itself as a cut. That's what makes two
separate shots read as one continuous kinetic gesture rather than an edit.

Example: "whip pan right off the runner's face as she turns toward the
finish line, blur-to-blur cut into the finish line shot already in motion."

Counter-example: a whip pan across a static, empty scene with nothing
motivating the turn — the speed reads as arbitrary and calls attention to
itself as an edit trick instead of disappearing into the cut.
