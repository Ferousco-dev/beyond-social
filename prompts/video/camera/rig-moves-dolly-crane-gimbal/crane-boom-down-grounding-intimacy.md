A crane descent from a high, wide vantage down to eye level or below places
the viewer into a scene as if they just arrived, the inverse use of the same
rig that booms up for closure.

- Start wide enough that the descent has real vertical distance to travel;
  a short drop reads as a tilt, not a crane move.
- Accelerate slightly through the middle of the descent, then ease into the
  landing framing so it doesn't feel like a controlled elevator.
- Land on a specific action already in progress: pouring coffee, tying laces,
  not an empty pose waiting for the camera.
- Match the landing frame size to whatever shot follows so the cut doesn't
  jump in scale.

Why: descending motion mimics the physical act of arriving somewhere, so
landing on an in-progress action places the audience inside a world that was
already running rather than one staged for the lens.

Example: "crane descends from a wide rooftop view down to street level,
landing at eye height on a barista steaming milk, ending in a static medium
shot."

Counter-example: descending onto a subject standing idle and posed for
camera — since nothing was already happening, the landing has no destination
and the move reads as the camera finding an empty mark.
