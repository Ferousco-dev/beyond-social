A crane or jib rising vertically while the frame widens shrinks the subject
relative to their environment, which the visual language of film has coded as
scale, status, or an ending.

- Pair the rise with a wide lens (24-35mm equivalent) so the widening frame
  reads as scale, not distortion.
- Decelerate into the final held frame; real crane operators ease out over
  the last 10-15% of travel rather than stopping hard.
- Use it once, near the close of a scene or spot, not as a repeatable
  transition — its meaning depends on scarcity.
- Give the rise something to climb past: a ceiling, a skyline, a crowd, so the
  widening has visible content, not empty sky.

Why: rising above eye level breaks the default human viewing height, and film
grammar has used that specific break for a century to shift the audience from
"with the character" to "seeing the character's place in the world." That
shift is what reads as awe or finality.

Example: "crane rises from eye-level on the founder to a wide view of the
warehouse floor, rise decelerating into a static wide shot, 6 seconds."

Counter-example: booming up mid-scene with no narrative build toward it —
technically smooth, but it breaks the viewer's spatial model and reads as
decorative drone-style flourish, a known synthetic-video default.
