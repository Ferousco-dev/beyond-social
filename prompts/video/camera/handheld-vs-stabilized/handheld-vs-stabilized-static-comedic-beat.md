Comedic and deadpan beats depend on the camera doing nothing. A static frame
lets the audience's eye land on the exact moment a sight gag or reaction lands,
without the camera stealing or softening the beat.

- Lock the shot before the gag starts and hold through the punchline with no
  reframe, push, or cut.
- Frame wide enough to contain the entire physical action of the joke, a prop
  failing, a reaction, so nothing exits frame mid-beat.
- Resist punching in on a reaction; a cut or push telegraphs "this is the joke"
  and steals the audience's own read of the moment.
- Hold slightly longer than feels necessary after the gag lands; comedic timing
  needs that half-beat of stillness to register.

Why: camera movement is itself a form of editorial emphasis, so any move during
a punchline pre-announces that the moment matters, which flattens the surprise.
The classic sitcom and deadpan-ad technique is to let stillness carry the
surprise instead of the camera doing it.

Example: "locked-off wide shot, the product comically fails in frame, hold two
full seconds after with no camera move."
Counter-example: "quick push-in the instant the joke lands" — undercuts the
timing by visually announcing the punchline before the viewer processes it.
