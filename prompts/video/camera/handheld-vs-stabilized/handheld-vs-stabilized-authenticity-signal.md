Handheld imperfection reads as authentic because it encodes evidence of a human
operator holding a physical object and reacting in real time, not because shake
alone equals "real."

- Give the shake a single operator-body cause: a bob synced to implied footsteps
  or breath, not disconnected random noise layered on top of the frame.
- Build in reaction lag: let the frame catch up to the subject a beat late (they
  step toward the edge, the camera corrects after) rather than pre-empting it.
- Cluster the biggest correction in the first half-second of a shot, then settle;
  a real hand overshoots on arrival and self-corrects, it doesn't start locked.
- Keep amplitude low, a few degrees of roll and pitch drift, not visible bounce;
  authenticity comes from the pattern, not the size of the wobble.

Why: viewers have an implicit model, built from a lifetime of phone video, of
what a hand-operated camera looks like reacting to unpredictable motion. A
generated shot that tracks perfectly with zero lag violates that model even when
the visual style is otherwise convincing, because no human hand anticipates
motion it hasn't seen yet.

Example: "handheld phone shot, slight late correction as she steps into frame,
camera settles about half a second after she stops."
Counter-example: "handheld camera perfectly tracking her fast, unpredictable
movement with zero lag" — the impossible anticipation reads as synthetic no
matter how convincing the shake texture looks.
