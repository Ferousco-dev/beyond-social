Testimonial interviews are conventionally edited with visible jump cuts
within one continuous shot setup, to compress rambling answers into tight
statements. The jump cut itself is a genre marker of "unscripted real talk,"
not a mistake to hide.

The recipe:

- Cut within the same framing and eyeline, not to a new angle, so the jump
  reads as trimmed time rather than a scene change.
- Keep each retained segment short, roughly one complete thought, three to
  eight seconds, before the next jump.
- Let a small continuity mismatch show through occasionally, a slightly
  different head position or blink; a perfectly matched jump cut can look
  uncannily over-edited.
- Reserve a second angle or a b-roll cutaway specifically for jumps where the
  mismatch is too jarring, rather than using it on every cut.
- Keep pacing responsive to content: tighten pauses and filler, but preserve
  one natural breath between statements.

Why: audiences are now fluent in the testimonial jump-cut grammar from years
of creator content and case-study videos. A smooth, seamless cut across a
monologue can paradoxically feel more edited or scripted than a visible jump,
because the jump signals "we trimmed a real, longer conversation" rather than
"we staged one short one."

Example: "jump cut mid-sentence to the next thought, same framing, slight
natural mismatch in head position."

Counter-example: cutting every few seconds to a different camera angle to
hide all the jumps — the constant angle-switching reads as an overproduced
multi-cam interview, not a candid one.
