Lifestyle/testimonial sits between UGC and cinematic on every axis at once: it
borrows realism from UGC and production control from cinematic, without fully
committing to either.

The recipe, axis by axis:

- Stability: smoother than UGC's handheld shake but not tripod-locked or
  dolly-precise — a gimbal-smoothed handheld with a slight organic drift.
- Light: motivated and available like UGC (windows, lamps), but shaped with a
  bounce or reflector like a small crew would, not raw phone flare.
- Performance: conversational and unscripted-sounding like UGC, but framed and
  paced with the composure of an interview subject, not a diary-video subject.
- Grade: true-to-life color like UGC, with a light consistency pass, never
  UGC's uncorrected white balance and never cinematic's teal-orange stylization.
- Net read: "someone helped them look good," not "they filmed it themselves"
  and not "an agency produced this."

Why: testimonial advertising has to satisfy two beliefs simultaneously —
that the person and place are real (UGC's job) and that the statement was
considered enough to trust (cinematic's job). Splitting the difference on
every technical axis is what encodes both beliefs in the footage itself,
rather than leaning on either extreme and losing one belief to gain the other.

Example: "real person seated in her living room, gimbal-smoothed handheld
interview shot, window light bounced with a reflector, true-to-life color
with a gentle contrast lift."

Counter-example: a raw front-camera selfie video reads as UGC diary content,
not testimony; a three-point-lit interview against a seamless backdrop reads
as a corporate case-study video, not a lifestyle testimonial. Both miss the
midpoint this style depends on.
