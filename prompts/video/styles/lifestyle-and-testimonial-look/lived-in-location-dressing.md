The believability of a testimonial rests more on set dressing than on the
performance: a real kitchen or office has specific, asymmetric clutter, not
staged minimalism.

The recipe:

- Populate visible surfaces with a few specific, imperfect objects: a mug with
  a ring stain, mail on the counter, a jacket slung over a chair back.
- Keep arrangement asymmetric; real rooms are not catalog-styled with matched
  spacing and centered objects.
- Include at least one object that signals a life beyond the product itself —
  a kid's drawing on the fridge, a dog bed, a half-read paperback.
- Keep the product present and in use, not centered on an empty surface like
  an altar display.
- Match the clutter to the claimed persona: a "busy parent" testimonial needs
  different mess than a "freelance designer" one.

Why: real domestic and work space accretes evidence of use over time, so a
viewer's eye reads staged emptiness as fake almost instantly; over-dressed
minimal environments are one of the fastest tells that a scene was built for
a camera rather than lived in.

Example: "her home office: a coffee cup, sticky notes on the monitor, a
cardigan on the chair, laptop open to actual work, the product visible on the
desk mid-use."

Counter-example: an empty white desk with only the product centered and
nothing else in frame — this reads as a product photography setup wearing a
testimonial's clothes, not an actual home.
