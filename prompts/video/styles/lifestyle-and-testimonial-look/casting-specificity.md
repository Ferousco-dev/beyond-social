Describe testimonial subjects as specific individuals with a defined context,
not as a demographic label — specificity is what makes a viewer believe a
stranger's statement.

The recipe:

- Write the subject as a specific person with a role and situation, "a
  night-shift nurse getting ready before a 7pm shift," not a generic label
  like "a woman."
- Avoid stock commercial-model polish, professional styling, symmetric
  features, camera-trained ease; prefer a plausible, slightly asymmetric,
  real-feeling presence.
- Match age, setting, and manner exactly to the claimed use case, not an
  aspirational upgrade of it.
- Give the subject something specific to be doing, or to have just finished,
  when the testimonial starts, so context precedes the pitch.
- Avoid aspirational-ad adjectives ("radiant," "flawless") in the casting
  description; describe the subject the way a documentary casting brief
  would.

Why: persuasive testimonials work because the viewer sees someone who could
plausibly be them or their neighbor. Casting language that drifts toward
commercial-model description quietly reintroduces the polished spokesmodel
look the entire format exists to move away from, even if every other
technical choice is correct.

Example: "a night-shift nurse in scrubs, mid-30s, sitting on her kitchen
counter eating cereal before her shift, talking between bites."

Counter-example: "an attractive woman with perfect skin smiling warmly at the
camera" — commercial-casting language that quietly undoes the format's
premise.
