Wardrobe should signal "dressed for their actual day," not "dressed for
camera": specific, slightly imperfect, occasion-appropriate clothing beats
coordinated, on-brand styling.

The recipe:

- Choose clothing appropriate to the claimed context: loungewear for a
  morning-routine testimonial, a blazer over a t-shirt for a "working from
  home" one.
- Never match the wardrobe color to the product's brand palette; coordinated
  color is a reliable advertising tell.
- Include one small imperfection: a slightly rumpled collar, sleeves pushed
  up, hair not freshly styled.
- Keep patterns and textures simple enough not to strobe on camera, but not
  showroom-pressed either.
- Vary wardrobe by subject across a set of testimonials so the cast does not
  read as a uniform.

Why: coordinated, color-matched wardrobe is one of advertising's most
reliable "this was produced" signals, and viewers who have seen thousands of
ads pattern-match on it almost instantly, often without consciously naming
what tipped them off. Deliberately avoiding brand-color coordination protects
the testimonial's credibility more than any other single wardrobe choice.

Example: "a slightly rumpled henley, sleeves pushed up, the kind of thing
someone actually wears on a Tuesday morning."

Counter-example: a pressed shirt in the exact shade of the product's logo
color — this reads as wardrobe styled by the brand, not chosen by the person.
