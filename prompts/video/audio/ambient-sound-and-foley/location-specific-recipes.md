Swap in a genuinely specific sound recipe for the location instead of a
generic "ambient noise" tag, because each environment carries a signature
combination a viewer recognizes instantly.

- Café: espresso machine hiss and steam, cup clinks, low overlapping
  voices, an occasional door chime.
- Office: keyboard clatter, HVAC hum, a distant phone ring, a chair roll.
- City street exterior: a traffic layer at distance, footsteps of
  passersby, an occasional horn, wind.
- Home kitchen: fridge compressor, faucet drip, a distant TV murmur
  through a wall.

Build each recipe from three or four named elements rather than one, since
a single sound (just "traffic," just "keyboard clicks") reads as thin and
staged, while a small combination reads as a place a microphone actually
sat in.

Why: audiences carry an internal library of what these spaces sound like
from lived experience. A mismatched or overly generic bed, office ambience
under a café shot, creates a subtle disagreement between eye and ear that
undercuts the location even when the visual set dressing is accurate.

Example: "office interior, keyboard clatter nearby, HVAC hum, a phone
ringing two desks over, no music."

Counter-example: labeling every interior scene "ambient background
noise," which generation tools resolve into the same generic room tone
regardless of whether it is a café, office, or bedroom.
