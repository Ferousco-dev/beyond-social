Clothing and fabric produce audible friction sound whenever a body moves,
and omitting it makes movement look like it is happening in a vacuum.

- Loose fabric, a jacket or coat, produces a soft rustle on arm swings and
  turns.
- Stiff fabric, denim or leather, produces a sharper, more textured
  friction sound.
- Sitting down or crossing legs should carry an audible fabric
  compression or shift.
- Scale rustle volume to garment weight and movement speed, not a flat
  constant hiss under every motion.
- Layer a faint fabric shift under seated dialogue scenes, since even
  stillness includes small postural adjustments that brush cloth against
  cloth.

Why: fabric-on-body and fabric-on-fabric friction is one of the quietest
but most consistently present real-world sounds. Its total absence during
visible motion is subtle enough that most viewers will not name it, but it
still reads as "something's off," because motion without any friction
sound plays as frictionless, like footage rendered rather than filmed.
Nylon, denim, and wool each have a distinct friction signature, and
naming the fabric gives the model something concrete to render instead of
a generic swish.

Example: "she turns toward camera, jacket sleeve brushing softly against
her side as her arm swings."

Counter-example: a character in a heavy coat walking briskly with zero
fabric sound under otherwise detailed foley, leaving the coat looking
weightless.
