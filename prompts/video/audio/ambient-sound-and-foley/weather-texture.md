Weather visible on screen needs a continuous, weather-appropriate texture
bed underneath it, because visible weather paired with silent air is one
of the more jarring mismatches a viewer will catch.

- Rain: a layered texture of drops on the nearest hard surface, roof,
  window, pavement, plus a wider hiss for distance.
- Wind: a variable-intensity whoosh or rustle tied to visible movement of
  trees, hair, or fabric on screen.
- Snow: near-total quiet is correct, but pair it with muffled,
  closer-sounding ambience, since snow dampens reflections, rather than
  dead silence.
- Match the texture's intensity to the visible severity, light drizzle
  versus downpour, and let it fluctuate rather than sit at one static
  level.

Why: weather is one of the most acoustically active phenomena in the real
world, and viewers carry strong embodied memory of what rain, wind, and
snow sound and feel like. Silent or flat-level weather is immediately
legible as unconvincing even to a casual viewer.

Example: "steady rain on the window, individual drops audible against the
glass, a lower rumble of heavier rain on the roof further off."

Counter-example: visible heavy rain outside a window with the interior
audio otherwise clean and dry, no drop texture or muffled acoustic shift
at all.
