Convincing scene sound is built from at least two audible layers, a steady
background bed and specific foreground detail events, not one flat
ambience track.

- Bed: the constant, low, non-eventful layer that establishes the space,
  traffic hum, wind, HVAC.
- Foreground events: discrete, occasional sounds that vary and interrupt
  the bed, a door somewhere else, a dog barking, a car passing.
- Keep foreground events sparse and irregular, never evenly spaced or
  obviously looped.
- Hold the bed continuous across cuts within the same scene so a cut does
  not reset the sense of space.
- Give the bed and the foreground events different apparent distances, the
  bed diffuse and wide, foreground events closer and more defined, so the
  layers read as separate rather than collapsing into one texture.

Why: real environments are never a single flat sound. They are a floor of
ambient noise punctuated by unpredictable, spatially placed events, and it
is that irregular top layer that convinces a listener a space is inhabited
and three-dimensional rather than a looped stock texture. A single-layer
bed also tends to loop audibly once it runs past a few seconds, since
there is nothing on top to break up the ear's expectation of repetition.

Example: "café interior, steady espresso-machine and murmur bed, with an
occasional clinking cup and a chair scrape from off-frame."

Counter-example: one continuous, evenly-looped café ambience track with no
foreground variation, which the ear parses as a loop within seconds.
