The pitch, sharpness, and decay of an impact sound communicates the
weight and material of the object being handled or dropped, and a
mismatched impact sound undercuts the physics of the shot.

- Heavy or dense object, a book, stone, metal tool: low-pitched, short,
  resonant thud with minimal bounce.
- Light or hollow object, a paper cup, cardboard box: higher-pitched,
  quicker decay, sometimes a brief secondary rattle.
- Soft object, a fabric bag, pillow: dull, muffled contact sound with
  almost no ring.
- Match the follow-through sound, settling, rolling, bouncing, to the
  object's actual mass, not a generic single hit.
- Tie the sound's energy to the visible drop height and force, a gentle
  set-down and a dropped object should not share the same impact volume.

Why: viewers infer weight primarily through motion and sound together,
not sight alone. A small box that produces a deep, heavy thud on impact
reads as physically wrong even if its fall looked fine, because the ear is
doing physics the eye cannot. This is especially unforgiving in product
shots, where the impact sound is often the only cue a viewer gets for how
solid or premium an object actually is.

Example: "he drops the stack of hardcover books on the desk, a heavy,
short thud with no bounce, papers underneath rustling slightly."

Counter-example: a heavy suitcase set down with a light, hollow tap
sound, which contradicts the visible effort of carrying it and breaks the
physical read of the shot.
