Any object a hand touches on screen needs its own contact sound, because an
unscored touch is the fastest tell that a hand is not really touching
anything.

- Coffee cup set down: a specific clink of ceramic on wood or tile, not a
  generic thud.
- Keys, zippers, buttons, paper: each has a distinct micro-sound the moment
  it is manipulated.
- Match the sound to the material pairing involved: metal-on-metal,
  glass-on-marble, plastic-on-laminate each ring differently.
- Time the contact sound to the exact frame of contact, not a beat before
  or after the visible touch.
- Give lift and release their own quieter secondary sounds, a soft suction
  release when a phone lifts off a table, a faint slide when a card is
  picked up, not just the initial set-down.

Why: humans process touch and sound together from infancy, and the brain
expects a contact event to carry an acoustic signature. A silent or
generic touch reads instantly as puppetry rather than a person interacting
with real matter, no matter how well the hand is animated. In product
shots this matters even more, since the handling sound is often doing the
work of communicating build quality that the picture alone cannot convey.

Example: "he sets the ceramic mug down on the wooden counter, a short
clink followed by a faint wood resonance."

Counter-example: a hand reaches for a glass and lifts it with no audible
contact at all, an otherwise well-lit, well-framed shot collapses into an
obvious render the moment that touch stays silent.
