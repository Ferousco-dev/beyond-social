When a sentence spans a cut to a new shot, the vocal energy and pace should
carry across the cut unbroken, so the audio is the thread that makes two
different images feel like one continuous moment.

The recipe:

- Identify sentences that are meant to span a visual cut deliberately (as
  opposed to sentences that should end before a cut) and keep those as one
  unbroken breath group with no pause at the cut point.
- Match volume and pitch level on either side of the cut within the same
  utterance — no re-take-driven jump in tone, since the ear will register any
  inconsistency as two separate performances stitched together.
- Use this technique specifically to bridge a location or subject change that
  would otherwise feel disjointed, letting the constant voice smooth over the
  visual discontinuity.
- Reserve it for one or two moments per script; if every cut is bridged this
  way, the edit loses its ability to use silence as its own punctuation.

Why: continuity of voice is a cheaper and more reliable tool than any visual
match-cut technique for making two unrelated shots feel connected, because
speech is processed as a single continuous stream regardless of what the eye is
seeing — an unbroken vocal line effectively tells the brain "this is still one
thought," even across a hard visual cut.

Example: "...and that's why we rebuilt it from scratch" continues unbroken
across a cut from the old interface to the new one, same pace, same energy.
Counter-example: the sentence is awkwardly split with a pause exactly at the
cut, which reads as two separate takes glued together rather than one thought.
