Stress belongs on the word that carries new or contrastive information, almost
never on the verb or the article, and identifying that word is a script-writing
decision, not something to leave to a default TTS prosody model.

The recipe:

- Find the one word per sentence that would change the meaning if swapped out
  ("it's not faster, it's cheaper") and mark that as the stress target.
- Write the line so the benefit word lands late, near the end of the breath
  group, where stress is easiest for a listener to register.
- Underline or bold that single word in the script sent to a voice actor or used
  as a TTS emphasis tag; do not mark more than one word per sentence.
- Say the sentence twice with the stress on two different candidate words and
  pick whichever version actually changes the meaning heard.

Why: English marks meaning through pitch and loudness on one syllable per
phrase (contrastive stress), and listeners parse that stress automatically to
find "the point" of a sentence. If the stress falls on a filler word by default,
the sentence sounds correct but says nothing — the actual selling proposition
never gets marked as important.

Example: "It's not another to-do app, it's the one that actually finishes tasks
FOR you" — stress on "for."
Counter-example: an unmarked TTS read that evenly stresses "actually finishes
tasks for you," burying the one word that makes the claim distinct.
