The words on the page determine the pacing available to the voice; a script
written in formal, written-register prose will produce a stiff read no matter
how well it's performed, because the sentence shapes themselves aren't the
shapes people speak in.

The recipe:

- Use contractions by default (it's, that's, you're) — spelling them out
  ("it is") is a written-register choice that forces an artificially formal
  cadence even from a good reader.
- Write sentence fragments where a real speaker would use them. "Not bad for
  ten bucks." is a complete spoken thought even though it isn't a complete
  grammatical sentence.
- Avoid stacked subordinate clauses ("which, when combined with, allows users
  to..."); split them into separate short spoken beats instead.
- Read every line out loud while drafting, not just when the VO is recorded —
  if a phrase catches in your own mouth, rewrite it before it reaches the
  voice actor or TTS engine.

Why: written and spoken English are genuinely different registers with
different rhythms, and a script that reads well silently on the page can still
be nearly unspeakable, because formal syntax forces pauses and stresses in
places natural speech never puts them. Writing for the ear first is what gives
a voice actor or TTS model material it can actually deliver naturally.

Example: "Not gonna lie, this fixed my whole morning routine." reads exactly
like something a person would say.
Counter-example: "This product has significantly improved my daily morning
routine efficiency," which is grammatically fine and almost unspeakable as a
natural, casual read.
