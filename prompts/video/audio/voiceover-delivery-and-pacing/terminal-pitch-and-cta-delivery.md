A call to action needs a falling pitch contour that ends below the speaker's
resting pitch, because a rising or level ending reads as a question or an
unfinished thought, not an instruction.

The recipe:

- Land the final word of the CTA on a pitch noticeably lower than where the
  sentence started — a genuine downward glide, not a flat monotone stop.
- Slow the final 2-3 words by roughly 20% relative to the sentence's average
  rate; deliberateness reads as confidence.
- Avoid uptalk (a rise on the last syllable) even when the CTA is phrased as a
  soft suggestion like "you might want to check it out" — the phrasing can be
  soft, the pitch contour should not be.
- Give the CTA its own breath group with a clean stop after it — no trailing
  words tacked on after the falling pitch has already landed.

Why: falling terminal pitch is how spoken English marks a completed, confident
statement; rising terminal pitch marks a question or hedged uncertainty. A CTA
delivered with rising or flat pitch subconsciously reads as the speaker asking
permission rather than directing the viewer, which measurably softens the ask.

Example: "Get it before it's gone." delivered with pitch dropping and pace
slowing across "before it's gone."
Counter-example: the same line read with a slight rise on "gone" — technically
the right words, but it sounds like a question the viewer could say no to.
