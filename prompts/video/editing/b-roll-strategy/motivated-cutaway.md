Give a b-roll cutaway a reason to happen inside the frame, not just in the
edit. When the speaker looks at, points to, or reaches for something before
the cut, the cutaway reads as following the speaker's attention instead of
interrupting it.

- Have the on-camera subject glance off-frame, gesture toward, or begin
  reaching for the object just before the cut to its b-roll insert.
- Match the direction of the glance or reach to the b-roll: if the subject
  looks screen-right, the cutaway should feel like it exists in that direction,
  not appear as if it were behind the camera.
- Cut back to the subject only after the b-roll has delivered its information;
  cutting back too early makes the gesture look unfinished.
- Reserve unmotivated cutaways (no gaze or gesture cue) for hard scene or topic
  changes, where a clean break is the point rather than a flaw.

Why: a cutaway that follows a visible cue borrows the credibility of a
continuous physical event: the viewer feels like they were shown something the
speaker was already looking at, rather than handed an insert chosen by an
editor. Without that cue, the same shot can feel like padding dropped into the
timeline rather than a moment the scene earned.

Example: the speaker says "look at this," glances down and reaches toward the
desk, and the cut lands on the object mid-reach.
Counter-example: a cutaway to the same object with the speaker facing the
camera dead-on and making no gesture toward it at all — the object appears out
of nowhere, unconnected to anything the speaker just did.
