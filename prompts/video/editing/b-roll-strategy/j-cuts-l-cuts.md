Stagger where the audio cuts and where the picture cuts so b-roll transitions
feel motivated by what's being said rather than mechanically synced to it.

- J-cut: bring in the b-roll's sound, or the next VO line, 0.3-0.8s before its
  picture appears, so audio leads picture into the cutaway.
- L-cut: let the current shot's audio (VO or sync sound) continue 0.5-1s after
  the picture has already cut to the b-roll, so the voice stays "in the room"
  while the proof shot plays.
- Use J-cuts to set up a location or action just before revealing it visually;
  use L-cuts to keep the speaker's authority present while the evidence shows.
- Carry a thin layer of ambient or room tone under every cut point. A hard
  audio cut under a soft video cut is one of the fastest amateur tells.

Why: a hard cut, where audio and video change on the identical frame, is the
default output of most template-driven or automated assembly, and it reads as
mechanically synced rather than handled. Staggering audio and picture cuts is
standard documentary and narrative technique, and it is one of the cheapest,
least visible ways to make an edit feel authored rather than assembled.

Example: VO says "...so we rebuilt the onboarding flow," and the audio for that
line starts half a second before the cut leaves the speaker's face for the new
flow on screen.
Counter-example: every cut lands exactly on a sentence boundary, audio and
picture together, clip after clip — technically clean, but the rhythmic
regularity of it is itself a tell of automated assembly.
