Cut points land better on an audio transient, a plosive consonant, a
stressed syllable, or a beat hit, than at a round timecode number, because the
ear notices a mismatch before the eye does.

- Find the transient in the waveform, a spike from a hard consonant like "p,"
  "t," "k," or a beat hit, and place the cut within a couple of frames of it.
- On scripted lines, cut on the emphasized word, not the one before or after
  it, so the cut reinforces meaning instead of just chopping time.
- When cutting to music, quantize hard cuts to the beat grid; off-beat cuts
  read as sloppy even when the visuals are fine.
- Across a run of quick cuts, keep audio and video edits at the same point,
  within 1-2 frames, unless a split edit is deliberately intended.

Why: hearing resolves timing faster than vision. An audio splice even a few
frames off from its visual cut registers as an error before the viewer can
name what's wrong, while syncing to a transient gives the cut a reason to
exist that the ear confirms rather than questions.

Example: "cut exactly on the hard 't' in 'stop wasting money' — the plosive
masks the splice."

Counter-example: cutting on a whole-second timecode mark in the middle of a
held vowel, producing an audible pop or warble because the waveform wasn't
split at a natural consonant break or zero crossing.
