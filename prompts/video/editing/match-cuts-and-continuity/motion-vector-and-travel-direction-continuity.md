Screen direction alone is not enough: the eye also tracks rate of motion, and
independently generated clips will often agree on left-to-right travel while
silently disagreeing on how fast, producing a stutter even when direction is
correct.

- Establish a subject's travel direction and speed once, such as "walking left to
  right at a relaxed pace, roughly 1.2 meters per second," and hold both across
  every cut within that journey.
- Check apparent speed at the cut point specifically, not just direction; a brisk
  walk cut into a slow saunter reads as a jump even with matched screen direction.
- For vehicles or fast subjects, match motion-blur amount and the implied shutter
  angle between clips so velocity feels physically continuous.
- Change speed only with a motivated transition, such as a speed ramp or a clear
  reason for the subject to stop or run.

Why: velocity is a physical quantity the body tracks kinesthetically while
watching motion; a mismatch in rate, not just direction, is felt as a stutter even
by viewers who could not describe what changed.

Example: "brisk walk, screen-left to screen-right, roughly 1.2 meters per second"
specified identically for both the wide shot and the medium shot that follows it.
Counter-example: a jogging pace in the establishing shot cut into an unhurried
stroll in the very next shot of what is meant to be the same walk.
