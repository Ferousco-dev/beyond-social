An audience reads camera movement as a proxy for a human operator's presence, so
when that "operator's" behavior changes unmotivated between cuts, the illusion of
one coherent shoot breaks even if every other element matches.

- Decide the camera's movement type once per scene, static, slow push-in, or
  handheld drift, and hold it; do not cut from a locked tripod shot into a push-in
  unless it is a deliberate scale change.
- If a push-in spans a cut, match its rate: the framing size gained by the end of
  clip A should be close to where clip B logically continues from.
- Match handheld character, the amplitude and frequency of the shake, across
  clips meant to feel like one operator's continuous take.
- Reverse or stop a camera move only on a motivated beat, such as an impact or a
  reveal, not as a side effect of separate generation calls defaulting differently.

Why: viewers do not consciously track camera behavior, but they register a shift
in it as a change in who or what is filming, which breaks the sense of a single
continuous shoot even when the subject and setting are otherwise identical.

Example: "slow push-in, roughly five percent frame-size gain per second"
specified identically for both halves of a push that spans the cut.
Counter-example: a static locked-off clip cut directly into a whip-pan of the same
subject with no motivated reason for the sudden camera move.
