Named grades function as a visual genre vocabulary audiences already read
fluently: teal-orange signals action blockbuster, bleach bypass signals
war or thriller, pastel high-key signals clean beauty. The grade should be
chosen from the brief's emotional register and category convention, not from
whichever preset looks most "cinematic."

The lookup logic:

- Energy, action, tech: teal-orange if the mood is aggressive, neo-noir if
  the mood is restrained and premium.
- Comfort, trust, testimonial: desaturated naturalism.
- Beauty, wellness, family: pastel high-key or golden hour warm.
- Nostalgia, irony, Gen-X audience: VHS retro.
- Tension, edge, streetwear: bleach bypass.
- Product accuracy, e-commerce: clean commercial, no stylized look.
- Always check the choice against competitors' existing use of the same look before committing.

Why: picking the wrong genre signal fights the brief's message even when
every individual grading move is executed well, because audiences decode
color grammar before they process a single word of copy. The right choice
lets one frame communicate genre instantly; the wrong one makes the viewer
untangle a contradiction between what they see and what they're told.

Example: "brief: premium skincare launch, trust-first tone, so desaturated
naturalism with a golden-hour warm bias, not teal-orange."

Counter-example: defaulting to teal-orange because it is the most common
cinematic preset, on a quiet, trust-building healthcare explainer — the
aggressive genre signal undercuts the calm, credible tone the brief needs.
