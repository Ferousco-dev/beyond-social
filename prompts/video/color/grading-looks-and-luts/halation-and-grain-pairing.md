A stylized color grade sitting on clean digital footage reads as an unconvincing
filter. Halation (the warm glow bleeding around bright highlights, caused by
light scattering through film's anti-halation backing) and matched grain are
what make a grade read as captured rather than applied.

The recipe:

- Add halation specifically around practical lights and specular highlights, not globally.
- Match grain size to the reference stock: fine for a Portra-style grade,
  coarser and clumpier for pushed 16mm or bleach-bypass looks.
- Grain should move with the frame at native resolution, not sit as a static overlay.
- Keep halation warm, red-orange; a cyan halation reads as broken, not filmic.

Why: without matching texture, a color grade is only a hue and contrast shift
riding on top of obviously clean sensor output. Viewers register the mismatch
between "film colors" and "digital cleanliness" subconsciously, even without
naming it, and that mismatch is one of the clearer tells of synthetic footage.

Example: "warm halation bleeding around the streetlights, fine organic grain
across the frame, matching a Portra-style grade."

Counter-example: applying a film LUT to a pristine, noise-free frame — the
colors shift but the underlying image still reads as digital.
