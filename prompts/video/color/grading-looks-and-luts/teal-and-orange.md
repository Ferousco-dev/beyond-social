Teal-and-orange pushes shadows and backgrounds toward cyan while holding skin
and key light warm, exploiting complementary contrast to add perceived depth
to a flat digital image. It fits high-energy, tech, and urban-night briefs; it
fights beauty, food, and quiet-testimonial briefs.

The recipe:

- Shift shadow-wheel hue toward 180-200 degrees (cyan-teal); leave highlights alone.
- Keep midtone and skin hue near 20-40 degrees (warm orange); protect it with a skin key.
- Cap saturation gain on the skin channel separately from the background gain.
- Never push highlights teal; it kills specular whites and reads as a filter, not lighting.

Why: eyes are trained to read warm skin as healthy and cool tones as distance or
depth, so cinematographers use warm/cool contrast to fake dynamic range a flat
sensor doesn't actually have. The moment the split stops being motivated by an
actual warm key light against a cool environment, it stops reading as lighting
and starts reading as a preset laid over the top.

Example: "urban night driving shot, warm tungsten skin tones on the driver, cool
teal in the wet street reflections and background neon."

Counter-example: applying a blanket teal-orange LUT to a beauty product close-up
— foundation shades shift cyan-grey and the shot reads as broken color, not styled.
