Colored light bouncing onto skin or a product from something visible in the
scene, a colored wall, a neon sign, saturated packaging, reads as real
precisely because it's motivated by a source the viewer can see, so any color
spill needs a visible or implied cause rather than appearing as an
unexplained tint.

- Only introduce a colored cast on skin or product if there's a plausible
  source in or near frame: a colored gel, a neon sign, a saturated wall,
  sunset light through colored glass.
- Keep the spill localized and falling off with distance and angle; spill
  should be strongest on the side of the face or product nearest the source
  and fade across the surface, not applied as a flat overlay.
- Match the spill's intensity to the source's plausibility: a small neon sign
  motivates a subtle rim of color, not a full-face wash.
- If no motivated source exists in the prompt, don't add a color cast for
  mood; use exposure, contrast, or composition instead.

Why: viewers unconsciously reason about where light in a shot comes from, so a
color cast with no visible cause reads as a filter or a grading choice rather
than something that happened in the room, while the same cast tied to a
visible neon sign or colored wall reads as environmental truth even though the
pixel values could be identical.

Example: "warm neon sign visible camera-left, its pink-orange spill falling
off across the near side of the face and product, fading by the far side."

Counter-example: a uniform magenta tint across the whole frame with no colored
light source anywhere in shot reads as an unmotivated filter rather than
something that actually happened in the space.
