Real glass is imperfect: light hitting the front element at an angle
scatters into flare, and high-contrast edges pick up faint color fringing,
both artifacts that are physically motivated by the lens rather than a
digital effect layered on top, and naming them beats asking for "cinematic
lighting" as a vague catch-all.

- Motivate the flare with an actual light source in frame or just out of
  frame: "low sun just outside the top of frame throws a soft warm flare
  across the upper third," not flare with no light source causing it.
- Keep it asymmetric and inconsistent shot to shot, since a real flare shifts
  as the camera or subject moves relative to the light; a flare that stays
  identically placed and shaped across a whole scene reads as a static
  overlay rather than an optical event.
- Mention chromatic aberration sparingly and only at genuine high-contrast
  edges: "a faint red-green fringe along the edge of the bright window
  frame," reserved for backlit or high-contrast situations where it would
  actually occur.
- Do not stack flare, aberration, film grain, and vignette all in one prompt
  by default; pick the one or two that suit the specific lighting situation
  described, since real footage doesn't carry every lens artifact in every shot.

Why: flare and aberration are downstream of a light ray physically passing
through curved glass elements, so tying them to an actual light source and
letting their strength vary with the scene is what makes them read as
optical truth rather than a preset filter pasted over an otherwise clean render.

Example: "late afternoon sun low behind the subject, warm flare streaking
across the frame as they turn, faint fringing along the sunlit edge of their hair."
Counter-example: "add lens flare for a cinematic look" with no light source
motivating it risks a flare that looks pasted on, disconnected from anything
in the scene that would actually cause it.
