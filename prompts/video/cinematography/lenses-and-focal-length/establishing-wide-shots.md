An establishing shot's job is to orient the viewer in a place, which favors
a genuinely wide focal length (16-28mm) and a smaller aperture that keeps
most of the scene legible, the opposite priority from a portrait or product
hero shot that wants to isolate one thing.

- Name both the focal length and a mid-to-small aperture together: "24mm at
  f/5.6," since a wide lens shot wide open still throws distant elements soft,
  undercutting the "show the whole place" purpose of the shot.
- Let the wide lens's natural depth exaggeration work for the shot: near
  objects (a doorway, a sign, a foreground plant) can loom larger, giving the
  frame a sense of layered depth rather than a flat wide view.
- Keep verticals honest: architecture and doorways should stay close to
  straight unless the wide-angle keystoning is a deliberate stylistic choice,
  since uncorrected keystoning on straight lines reads as an amateur phone
  shot rather than an intentional establishing shot.
- Hold the establishing shot for slightly longer than the following cuts;
  wide, deep-focus frames take longer for a viewer's eye to parse than a
  tight, one-subject shot.

Why: an establishing shot is doing spatial-orientation work, not emotional
isolation work, so the same shallow-depth-of-field instinct that helps a
product or portrait shot actively works against an establishing shot's job
of legibly showing where the story is happening.

Example: "24mm wide at f/5.6, storefront and sidewalk both legible, morning
light raking across the street, held for three seconds before cutting in."
Counter-example: "wide establishing shot" with a shallow-depth default (or
none stated) risks a soft, ambiguous background that fails to actually
establish the space the following shots are set in.
