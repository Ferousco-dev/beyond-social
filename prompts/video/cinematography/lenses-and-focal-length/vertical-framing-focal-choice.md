A 9:16 frame crops away most of the horizontal real estate that a focal
length's compression normally reads against, which means the same lens choice
looks different in vertical than it does in the horizontal reference most
cinematography vocabulary was built around, and the prompt should compensate.

- For a single subject filling a vertical frame, a slightly longer focal
  length than the horizontal equivalent (say 50mm instead of 35mm) reads
  correctly, because the crop already removes the wide-angle side context
  that a shorter lens would normally supply.
- For product-in-hand vertical shots, keep the lens in the 35-50mm range;
  going wider to fit more into the tall frame invites the barrel distortion
  and hand-size exaggeration that reads badly on close, static product shots.
- When the vertical frame needs to show full-body movement, favor a wider
  lens (24-28mm) but keep the camera farther back rather than closer, so the
  height of the frame is used for headroom and legroom, not distortion.
- State the framing intent alongside the lens ("50mm, vertical 9:16, subject
  fills frame chest-up with headroom") since focal length alone doesn't
  determine how a vertical crop uses the extra height.

Why: vertical video removes side context that horizontal compression
vocabulary assumes is visible, so a focal length chosen by horizontal habit
can put a subject too close to the lens for the frame's aspect ratio,
producing unintended distortion that a horizontal 35mm choice would not have.

Example: "50mm lens, vertical 9:16 frame, subject centered chest-up with
headroom above, background softly compressed behind them."
Counter-example: reusing "35mm" from a horizontal spec for a tall vertical
frame can push the camera closer to fill the height, exaggerating facial
proportions in a way the same lens wouldn't in landscape.
