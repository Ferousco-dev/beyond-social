Macro-length shots (effectively a magnified, close-focusing lens rather than a
standard focal length pulled tight) exist to show texture and material detail
that a normal working distance cannot resolve, and they carry their own
depth-of-field behavior that needs naming separately from a standard close-up.

- Name it as a macro insert, not just "close-up": "macro shot, extremely
  shallow depth of field, only the stitching on the seam in focus."
- Depth of field at macro distances is measured in millimeters, not feet, so
  state that the in-focus zone is a thin plane and everything even slightly
  in front of or behind it (not just the far background) falls soft.
- Give the camera or product a small, motivated movement across the focus
  plane (a slow pan across a fabric weave, a slight rack as a bead of
  condensation comes into focus) rather than a static frame, since real macro
  work is rarely perfectly locked off.
- Use macro inserts as short punctuation shots inside a product video, cut
  against wider shots that establish the whole product, not as the sole shot type.

Why: macro optics behave differently from a standard lens racked to its
closest focus distance, the depth of field collapses to millimeters and even
minor texture (fabric grain, condensation, brushed metal) becomes the entire
subject, so naming the macro distance and its razor-thin focus plane is what
separates a convincing detail shot from a merely close one.

Example: "macro insert, camera drifting slowly across the leather grain,
focus plane only millimeters deep, stitching sharp and the surrounding
surface falling immediately soft."
Counter-example: "extreme close-up on the fabric" without macro-specific
depth-of-field language risks a shot where the whole surface reads
uniformly sharp, missing the razor-thin focus plane that sells real macro work.
