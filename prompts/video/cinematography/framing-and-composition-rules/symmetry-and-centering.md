Center a subject exactly on a genuinely symmetrical set — matching architecture,
a product on an axis of light, a face square to the lens — and only then; a
centered subject against an asymmetrical or generic background reads as a
mistake rather than a formal choice.

The recipe:

- Verify the environment is actually symmetrical (doorway, two matching
  lights, a product's own bilateral shape) before centering the subject in it.
- Keep the camera height and angle locked square to the axis of symmetry; a
  tilted or off-height camera breaks the symmetry it's meant to showcase.
- Let small, real asymmetries survive inside the symmetrical frame — a stray
  hair, an uneven fold of fabric — so the shot still reads as photographed,
  not template-generated.
- Reserve this framing for moments that want to feel formal, ceremonial, or
  confrontational; it reads as cold if overused across a whole edit.

Why: true symmetry requires a symmetrical world to justify it; centering a
subject by default, with no matching environment, is exactly what an unguided
generator does, so deliberate symmetry has to be built from a real symmetrical
set to read as a choice instead of a default.

Example: "product centered on a symmetrical two-light setup, doorway frame
matching on both sides, camera locked square and level to the axis."
Counter-example: "subject centered against a random asymmetrical room" — no
visual logic supports the centering, so it reads as an unstyled default.
