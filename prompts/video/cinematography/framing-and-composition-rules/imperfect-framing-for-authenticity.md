A frame that is very slightly off — subject a few degrees from perfectly
level, not quite centered on the grid it's clearly aiming for, a tiny bit of
headroom miscalculated — reads as shot by a person, because no real camera
operator, even a skilled one, nails a mathematically perfect grid every take.

The recipe:

- Offset the subject a few percent from the exact third or center it's
  ostensibly aiming for; near-perfect beats exact.
- Allow a fraction of a degree of level tilt on a handheld or phone-style shot
  rather than a laser-level horizon.
- Let a shot start slightly mis-framed and settle (a small reframe or drift in
  the first half-second) rather than opening already locked to the final
  composition — real operators adjust after they hit record.
- Reserve pixel-exact grid alignment for shots that are meant to read as
  tripod-locked and formal (product hero shots, symmetrical brand moments);
  apply imperfection everywhere the brief calls for UGC, handheld, or documentary feel.

Why: exact compositional precision is a machine signature, not a human one —
professional camera operators compose by eye and get close, not exact, and
that small residual error is one of the more reliable perceptual cues that
separates footage a person captured from footage a system rendered to spec.

Example: "handheld phone framing, subject slightly left of the third line it's
aiming for, camera settles into position half a second into the shot."
Counter-example: "subject locked to the exact mathematical third-line
intersection for the full duration of a handheld-style shot" — the precision
contradicts the handheld premise.
