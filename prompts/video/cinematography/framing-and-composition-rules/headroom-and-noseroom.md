Leave a small, specific gap above the head (headroom) and in front of the face
in the direction a subject is looking or moving (nose room / lead room). Both
gaps have a correct range; too much or too little reads as a framing mistake
even when the rest of the shot is clean.

The recipe:

- Headroom: leave roughly 5-10% of frame height above the crown of the head for
  a medium shot; tighten to almost none for a big close-up, where a full head
  and forehead should nearly fill the top of frame.
- Nose room: when a subject's face or body is angled off-axis, shift the whole
  composition so there is more space in front of the face than behind it.
- For a talking avatar in a vertical frame, keep headroom tight (close to the
  hairline) since 9:16 has little vertical margin to spare above the shoulders.
- Never split the difference exactly evenly on both sides of a turned face —
  that reads as centered-by-default, not composed for a gaze direction.

Why: headroom and nose room encode where attention and implied motion are
going; a viewer reads "too much air above the head" as an accident and "no room
to look into" as claustrophobic, both of which break the illusion that a camera
operator made a deliberate choice.

Example: "medium close-up, subject looking camera-right, extra negative space
on the right of frame, tight headroom just above the hairline."
Counter-example: "subject centered with equal space left and right while
looking off to one side" — the gaze has nowhere to land, reads as a framing bug.
