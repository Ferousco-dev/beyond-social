Wherever a subject's eyes point, the viewer's attention follows; use that to
pull attention onto a product, a piece of on-screen text, or another person in
frame, rather than letting the gaze point at empty space or straight into a wall.

The recipe:

- If a product is in frame, have the subject glance toward it at least once
  before or after addressing the lens, not only at the camera the whole time.
- If on-screen text or a caption will land in a specific zone, have the
  subject's eyeline pass near that zone at some point in the take.
- In a two-shot or cutaway, match eyelines across cuts: if a subject looks
  camera-left at a person, the reverse shot should show someone looking
  camera-right back.
- Avoid a flat, unwavering stare at the lens for the full duration; real eyes
  make small saccades and occasional glances away, even in a direct-address shot.

Why: gaze direction is one of the strongest, most automatic attention cues
humans respond to, wired in well before language; a generated subject whose
eyes never move or never acknowledge the product it's supposed to be selling
breaks the implied logic of the scene even if every other detail is polished.

Example: "subject addresses camera, glances down-right toward the product on
the table, then returns to camera on the line 'and that's the difference.'"
Counter-example: "subject stares fixed at the lens for the entire ten-second
take while a product sits unacknowledged in frame" — the gaze contradicts the story.
