Group multiple objects or people in odd numbers — one, three, five — rather
than even numbers, because an odd group has no way to split into visually
paired-off halves and the eye is forced to settle on a center element.

The recipe:

- For product spreads, arrange three or five items rather than two, four, or
  six; place the visual anchor item in the middle of the group.
- For people in a shot, three reads as a natural cluster; two reads as a pair
  that needs equal weight, and four tends to visually split into two pairs.
- Vary the size or distance of each item slightly within the odd group so it
  doesn't read as a mechanically spaced row.
- When an even number is unavoidable (a duo interview, a matched product pair),
  break the tie with asymmetric spacing or staggered depth rather than a
  perfectly even line.

Why: an even count invites the eye to pair items off against each other with
no resolution, producing a low-level visual tension; an odd count always
leaves one item without a partner, and that item becomes the de facto focal
point without needing any other compositional signal.

Example: "three product bottles on a counter, staggered depth, center bottle
slightly forward and larger in frame."
Counter-example: "four identical bottles in a perfectly even row" — the eye
keeps splitting the row into two pairs with no clear anchor.
