Place a real foreground element — a doorway, a window, a gap between two
objects, an arm — around the edges of the shot so the subject sits inside a
second, smaller frame carved out of the main one.

The recipe:

- Use an out-of-focus foreground shape at the edges (a door frame, foliage, a
  shoulder) that occupies 10-20% of the frame's border, not the center.
- Keep the inner frame irregular, not a perfect rectangle; a real doorway is
  rarely centered or square to the lens.
- Let the foreground element be slightly underexposed or soft-focused relative
  to the subject, so it reads as physically closer to the lens.
- Use this device to justify a natural vignette instead of adding one in post;
  the darkening at the edges comes from an object, not a filter.

Why: a frame within a frame manufactures a third dimension (lens-to-foreground,
foreground-to-subject, subject-to-background) inside a single flat generated
image, and it mimics the incidental occlusions a real handheld camera picks up
by shooting through doorways and around objects rather than in a clear studio void.

Example: "shooting through a doorway, door edge soft and dark on the left third
of frame, subject in focus beyond it in a lit room."
Counter-example: "subject shot against a plain wall with an artificial vignette
added evenly around all four edges" — the darkening has no physical source.
