For a shot with a strong single point of interest and a curved or spiraling
element (smoke, hair, poured liquid, a curled cable, fabric), align that curve
to a golden-spiral path instead of a straight thirds grid, because the ratio
matches how the eye naturally traces a curve inward.

The recipe:

- Reserve this for shots that already contain an organic curved element; don't
  force a spiral grid onto a scene with no curved subject matter.
- Anchor the tight, small end of the spiral at the subject's focal point (a
  product cap, an eye, a flame).
- Let the wide end of the spiral open toward the edge of frame with the least
  important content, typically a soft background.
- Treat the golden ratio (about 1.618:1) as a looser guide than thirds, not a
  pixel-exact grid — real curved elements never trace a mathematically perfect
  spiral, and forcing one looks stiff.

Why: the golden spiral is a smoothly decreasing curve, and a smoothly
decreasing curve is a closer match to how attention actually decays as it
moves away from a focal point than the hard grid lines of thirds, which is why
it reads as more organic for compositions built around a curve rather than a
straight-edged subject.

Example: "steam curling up from a cup, spiral tightening toward the cup's rim
in the lower-left, opening toward soft background upper-right."
Counter-example: "forcing a golden spiral overlay onto a flat product-on-a-table
shot with no curved element to justify it" — the grid has nothing to trace.
