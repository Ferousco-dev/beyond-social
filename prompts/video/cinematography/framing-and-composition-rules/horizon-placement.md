Set the horizon on the upper or lower third line, never through the exact
vertical center, and choose which third based on what deserves more weight:
the ground plane or the sky.

The recipe:

- Horizon on the lower third when the sky, a product held up against it, or an
  open feeling is the point of the shot.
- Horizon on the upper third when the ground plane — a table surface, a street,
  a floor a product sits on — is what needs the visual weight.
- Keep the horizon level within roughly half a degree unless a tilt is a
  deliberate choice elsewhere in the shot; an unintentionally crooked horizon
  reads as an error, not a style.
- In a moving shot, let the horizon drift a pixel or two with natural handheld
  sway rather than locking it perfectly level throughout, which reads as
  gimbal-stabilized rather than optically real.

Why: a centered horizon splits the frame into two equal, competing halves with
no clear subject of gravity; pushing it to a third lets one half dominate and
gives the composition a single visual anchor, which is basic landscape and
product-photography grammar going back to painting.

Example: "horizon on the lower third, product held against open sky in upper
two-thirds, natural handheld micro-sway."
Counter-example: "horizon splitting the frame exactly in half" — sky and ground
fight for attention and neither wins.
