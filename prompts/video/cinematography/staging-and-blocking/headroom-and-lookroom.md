Headroom (space above the head) shrinks as the shot gets tighter, and lookroom
(space in front of the face, in the direction the subject is facing or looking)
must be biased toward whichever side the subject's gaze or body is angled toward.

- Close-up: minimal headroom, crop can touch just above the hairline; too much
  headroom on a close-up looks like an accidental zoom-out, not a choice.
- Medium shot: modest headroom, roughly one head-height of space above.
- Give more empty frame on the side the subject looks toward than the side
  behind their head; a subject looking into the frame edge reads as cramped.
- When a subject turns to address a product held up beside them, lookroom
  shifts to that side for the duration of the turn, not the original facing side.
- Do not center the face when the subject is angled three-quarters; the nose
  should not point at the frame's edge.

Why: lookroom exists because a viewer's eye unconsciously anticipates the space
a gaze is "using" — cutting that space off reads as visually claustrophobic,
even to viewers who could not name why. Headroom scales with shot size because
tighter shots are about the face itself, where empty space above becomes dead
weight, while wider shots need it to establish the person within an environment.

Example: "medium shot, subject facing camera-left, one head-height of headroom,
generous lookroom to the left where their gaze falls."
Counter-example: a tight close-up with a full head of empty space above and the
subject's nose nearly touching the right frame edge while looking left — both
headroom and lookroom are backward for the shot size and gaze.
