Staging distinct elements at three separate depths, rather than one subject
against a flat backdrop, is what makes a generated shot read as a real space
with volume instead of a cutout pasted over a background plate.

- Foreground: put one motivated object partially in frame (a plant edge, a door
  frame, a shoulder, steam from a cup) that the camera moves past or around.
  It should occupy the outer 10-15% of the frame, not compete for center focus.
- Midground: the actual subject or product, held in the sharpest focus and the
  clearest read of the three planes.
- Background: an environment with its own depth cues (receding hallway, window
  light falling off, blurred activity) rather than a single flat wall.
- Stagger the three planes at visibly different distances from camera, not
  clustered within a foot of each other, or the parallax reads as flat.
- Do not add a foreground element that isn't motivated by the scene; an
  unexplained blurry shape at frame edge reads as a rendering artifact.

Why: real cameras record parallax, the visible difference in how near and far
objects shift relative to each other during camera movement. A flat two-plane
composition (subject plus background) has no parallax to speak of, which is a
quiet but consistent signature of synthetic, stage-lit footage versus footage
shot in an actual room with actual clutter at varying distances.

Example: "foreground: out-of-focus doorway edge at left frame; midground: subject
seated at a desk, in focus; background: window with soft falloff, blurred street
motion beyond the glass."
Counter-example: subject shot against a single flat gradient backdrop with
nothing else in frame — dimensionally correct exposure, but no depth to read.
