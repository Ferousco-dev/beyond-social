A subject in motion needs more empty frame ahead of their direction of travel
than behind them, and the amount of that lead space should scale with how fast
they're moving, not stay fixed like static lookroom does.

- Walking pace: stage the subject roughly a third of the way back from the
  leading edge, two-thirds of frame open ahead of their path, since attention
  follows the direction of travel more than it lingers on where they started.
- Faster motion (running, a vehicle, a hand moving quickly toward the lens):
  push the subject further back in frame, leaving most of the frame as lead
  space, since the eye needs more runway to track fast motion comfortably.
- If the subject is about to exit frame on purpose (a walk-off transition),
  reduce lead space progressively across the shot rather than cutting it
  abruptly, so the exit reads as motivated rather than a framing error.
- Keep the trailing space behind the subject minimal and let it close in as
  they move, rather than holding the subject dead-center throughout the move.
- Do not center a moving subject with equal space front and back; it looks like
  the camera is politely refusing to acknowledge which way they're headed.

Why: viewers track a subject's implied path and want to see where it's going,
not just where it's been — a beat behind their motion is already resolved, but
the space ahead is where meaning is still unfolding, so it earns more of the
frame.

Example: "subject walking left to right at a brisk pace, staged in the left
third of frame, generous open space to the right along their path of travel."
Counter-example: a subject walking briskly but framed dead-center for the
entire shot — the motion reads as artificially contained rather than headed
somewhere.
