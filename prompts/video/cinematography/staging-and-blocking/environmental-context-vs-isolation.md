Where a product is staged, against a real environment or in an isolated studio
void, is a deliberate choice tied to what the shot needs to prove, and the two
are not interchangeable defaults for the same brief.

- Environmental staging (a kitchen counter, a bathroom shelf, a desk mid-use):
  use for proof-of-use, scale reference, and top-of-funnel discovery content,
  where the viewer needs to picture the product in their own life.
- Studio isolation (seamless backdrop, controlled reflection, no environmental
  clutter): use for detail call-outs, spec-driven consideration content, and
  any shot where the environment would compete with fine product detail.
- Keep environmental staging honest: real countertop wear, other objects in
  frame that a person would actually have nearby, not a styled, empty set
  dressed to look incidentally real.
- Keep studio staging clean but not sterile: a single motivated shadow and one
  consistent light source read as photographed, while shadowless, ambiguous
  lighting reads as a 3D render.
- Do not mix the two within one shot (a product on a real countertop but with
  a studio-seamless void immediately behind it); the two lighting and clutter
  logics contradict each other and the eye catches the seam.

Why: the two staging modes answer different viewer questions. Context answers
"does this fit my life," which matters early in a decision. Isolation answers
"what exactly does this look like," which matters once someone is already
close to buying. Picking the wrong one for the funnel stage weakens the ad even
if the footage itself is technically clean.

Example: "product staged on a lived-in kitchen counter, other everyday items
nearby, natural window light" for a discovery hook.
Counter-example: a product shot on a seamless studio backdrop used as the
opening hook for a cold-audience discovery ad — clinical and detail-forward
when the moment calls for relatable context.
