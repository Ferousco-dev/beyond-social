A reflective surface, a window, wet pavement, a mirror, a phone screen, adds
a genuine extra depth layer by showing content from a different part of
space superimposed on the frame, a compositional trick a flat backdrop
cannot fake.

- Choose a reflective surface that belongs to the scene, a storefront
  window, rain-wet street, a car window, and state what it reflects: passing
  traffic, the sky, a light source out of frame.
- Keep the reflection slightly softer, dimmer, or color-shifted from the
  direct-view elements; real reflections lose a stop or more of light and
  pick up the surface's tint, green-tinted glass, warm asphalt sheen.
- Use reflections to reveal information the direct camera angle doesn't
  show, what's behind the camera, a second character, rather than
  duplicating what's already visible, so the layer earns its place.
- Avoid a mirror-perfect, full-brightness reflection; that reads as a
  rendering trick rather than an imperfect real surface.

Why: reflections are a second optical path layered into one image, and
cinematographers use them deliberately, interrogation-room glass,
rain-streaked windshields, because they add spatial information for free.
Getting the physical imperfection right, dimmer, tinted, slightly distorted,
is what keeps a reflection from looking like a pasted-in duplicate layer.

Example: "shot through a rain-streaked cafe window, faint reflection of the
street's traffic lights layered dimly over the interior scene."

Counter-example: "a perfectly bright, mirror-sharp reflection duplicating
the scene exactly" has no light loss or distortion, and reads as a
copy-paste layer rather than real glass.
