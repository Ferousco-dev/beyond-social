Setting an explicit aperture or depth-of-field intent separates planes
optically, the way a real lens does, instead of leaving every plane in
identical crisp focus, which is a giveaway of synthetic render.

- Specify the f-stop character in plain terms: "shallow depth of field, f/1.8
  look" for creamy background blur, or "deep focus, f/8" only when the scene
  genuinely calls for everything sharp, such as a landscape establishing
  shot.
- Name which plane holds focus, "focus on the hands, background falls into
  soft bokeh," rather than leaving focus ambiguous.
- Ask for bokeh shape consistent with the implied lens and light source:
  round soft blur for a wide aperture, point-light bokeh for practical
  lights like string lights or headlights in the background.
- Avoid asking for "sharp and clear" as a blanket instruction; it collapses
  all planes into identical crisp focus, one of the biggest tells of AI
  flatness.

Why: circle-of-confusion physics means real lenses cannot hold infinite
depth at wide apertures. Viewers have a lifetime of photographic experience
that reads uniform sharpness across near and far as physically implausible,
while selective blur matches how cameras actually see.

Example: "shallow depth of field, f/2, focus locked on the espresso cup in
the foreground, background figures dissolve into soft round bokeh."

Counter-example: "everything in the shot perfectly sharp and in focus" is
physically implausible at close range and reads immediately as synthetic.
