A thin edge of light along a subject's silhouette, a rim or kicker from
behind or three-quarter-back, is the most reliable lighting technique for
separating a subject from a background of similar tone. Its absence is why
many generated subjects look glued onto their backdrop.

- Specify a backlight or three-quarter back light source with a plausible
  motivation: low sun, a doorway behind the subject, a streetlamp, a
  practical lamp just out of frame.
- Ask for the rim to hit hair, the shoulder line, and one side of the face
  or body, not a uniform halo around the entire silhouette, which reads as a
  compositing artifact.
- Keep the rim's color consistent with its named source, warm for tungsten
  or sunset, cool for overcast sky or a monitor glow, rather than a neutral
  white edge.
- Use rim light especially when subject and background sit at similar
  brightness or color; that is exactly when a real cinematographer would add
  one to keep the subject from reading flat against the wall.

Why: this is standard multi-point lighting grammar, key, fill, and back or
rim, used because cameras compress a three-dimensional scene onto a flat
sensor and lose depth information that focus alone doesn't fully restore at
a distance. A hard-edged rim reintroduces that separation optically instead
of leaving the model to guess at it.

Example: "late-afternoon sun behind the subject creates a warm rim along her
hair and shoulder, separating her from the dim interior background."

Counter-example: "evenly lit subject against a same-tone wall, no backlight"
puts subject and background at one brightness, and the silhouette dissolves
into the backdrop.
