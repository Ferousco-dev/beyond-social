Distant planes in the real world lose contrast, saturation, and edge
sharpness because light scatters through air. Naming that falloff explicitly
keeps a generated background from looking pasted onto the same depth as the
subject.

- Describe a graduated loss of contrast and saturation with distance:
  "foreground high contrast, distant hills desaturated and pale blue-gray."
- Reserve heavy haze for genuinely long sight lines, city skylines, mountain
  ranges, wide streets; a hazy midground three feet away is physically wrong
  and reads as a filter, not air.
- Pair haze with weather logic already stated in the prompt, a hot hazy
  afternoon, morning fog burning off, so the falloff has a stated cause.
- Combine with a slight desaturation and a whisper of blue-gray shift in
  distant elements, not a uniform gray wash laid over the whole background.

Why: Rayleigh and Mie scattering physically cause this falloff at real
distances, and viewers absorb it as a distance cue without consciously
noticing it. Its absence is one reason distant AI-generated backgrounds look
like a cardboard cutout or a printed backdrop dropped in at full contrast
right behind the subject.

Example: "foreground fruit stand in saturated color, midground street crisp,
distant skyline pale and desaturated in afternoon haze."

Counter-example: "distant mountains rendered in the same crisp saturated
detail as the foreground" has no distance falloff, so the background reads
as a flat backdrop print rather than miles of open air.
