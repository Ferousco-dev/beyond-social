Light traveling through a medium with particulate, dust, fog, steam, smoke,
becomes visible as shafts or beams, and those shafts mark out the empty air
between planes, turning invisible negative space into a legible layer of
depth.

- Name the particulate cause explicitly: dust motes in a sunbeam through a
  window, steam off a street grate, mist in a forest clearing, smoke from a
  cigarette or a grill.
- Angle the light source across or toward the camera, side or back light,
  rather than flat front light; volumetric shafts need a light path the lens
  can see edge-on.
- Keep density low to moderate for a grounded look; heavy uniform fog reads
  as a stage effect rather than found atmosphere.
- Let the shafts intersect a midground element, fall across a table, catch a
  figure walking through, so they visibly connect planes rather than
  floating decoratively in the background.

Why: this is genuine optical physics, light scattering off suspended
particles, and cinematographers rig haze machines specifically to make light
readable in a shot. Without particulate, light is invisible except where it
lands, so a generated shot skips a whole category of depth information a
real camera would otherwise capture.

Example: "morning sun through a warehouse window, dust hanging in the air, a
visible shaft crossing the room and catching the worker as she walks through
it."

Counter-example: "bright sunbeam through the window" with no haze or motes
described will likely render as a flat patch of light on the floor, not a
volumetric shaft carving through the space.
