Placing a physical object between the lens and the subject, a doorway edge,
foliage, a shoulder, a window mullion, creates an occluding layer that reads
as a real vantage point rather than a floating camera.

- Choose a frame element that belongs in the scene's geography: a door frame
  indoors, branches outdoors, a passing car street-side, not a generic
  vignette shape.
- Let it clip the edges of frame asymmetrically; a frame element centered and
  symmetrical reads as a designed border rather than something the camera
  happened to be behind.
- Keep the framing element soft or out of focus relative to the subject so it
  doesn't compete with the main action.
- Use it to justify camera position, "shooting from behind a curtain," which
  also motivates a slight camera height or angle that feels like a person
  standing somewhere, not a rig floating in space.

Why: real cinematography uses foreground elements constantly, doorframes,
foliage, rain-streaked glass, because the lens is a physical object sitting
in a physical space with things near it. Omitting any foreground occlusion
produces the hallmark "camera floating in a void" look common to default
generated shots.

Example: "shot through a gap in hanging vines, softly blurred in the
foreground, subject visible beyond in a jungle clearing."

Counter-example: "clean unobstructed shot of the subject in the jungle" puts
nothing between lens and subject, giving no sense the camera occupies real,
cluttered space.
