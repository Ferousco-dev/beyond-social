Shifting color temperature or saturation between planes, a warmer, more
saturated foreground against a cooler, more muted background, or the
deliberate reverse, is a grading decision independent of haze or focus that
still reads as spatial separation, and naming it prevents the render from
applying one flat color cast across the whole frame.

- State the direction explicitly: "warm practical light on the subject in
  the foreground, cooler ambient light in the background," common in
  interiors where tungsten sits near camera and daylight or fluorescent
  light sits further back.
- Use the reverse deliberately for specific moods: a cool, shadowed
  foreground against a warm background glow, a figure near a lit doorway,
  still separates planes with the temperature roles swapped.
- Keep the shift graduated across the midground rather than a hard line
  between two color zones, which reads as a compositing seam.
- Tie the temperature difference to a named light source per plane, a lamp,
  a window, a neon sign, so the color logic has a physical cause rather than
  floating free of the scene.

Why: real environments are almost never lit by one uniform color source;
mixed lighting, tungsten practicals near camera, daylight from a window
further back, neon spill in a background alley, is the normal case. A
single flat white balance across the whole frame is what a default render
produces when color per light source isn't specified.

Example: "warm lamp light on the subject's face in the foreground, the room
behind her cooling toward blue near the window."

Counter-example: "even, neutral white-balanced light across the whole scene"
puts a single color temperature over every plane, reading as one flat,
evenly lit set rather than a real mixed-light room.
