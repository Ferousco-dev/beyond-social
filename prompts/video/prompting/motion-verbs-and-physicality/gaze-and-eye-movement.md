Eye movement carries more information through its verb than through its target.
"Darts," "flicks," "settles on," and "tracks" each describe a different real
gaze behavior; "looks at" describes none of them.

The recipe:

- Use saccade verbs for quick attention shifts: darts, flicks, cuts to.
- Use settle or hold verbs for sustained attention: settles on, lingers, holds.
- Name what interrupts a gaze: glances away, breaks eye contact, checks over a
  shoulder.
- Sequence gaze ahead of the body: in real behavior, the eyes usually lead a
  head or body turn by a beat.

Why: eye behavior is one of the fastest tells viewers register for artificial
performance, often faster than they can articulate why a shot feels off. Real
human gaze is saccadic, rapid jumps between brief fixations, not a smooth glide
or a locked stare. "Looks at camera" defaults to an unbroken, centered,
unblinking stare, which is precisely the vacant quality that separates
synthetic-looking footage from a shot of an actual person reacting to something.

Example: "her eyes flick to the door, then settle back on him."
Counter-example: "she looks at him" — produces one long, static, centered gaze
held for the shot with no saccade and no interruption.
