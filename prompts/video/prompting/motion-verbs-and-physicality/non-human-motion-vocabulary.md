Liquids, smoke, fabric, and mechanical parts each have their own vocabulary of
motion verbs, distinct from body-motion verbs, and borrowing a human-motion verb
for them produces uncanny, wrong-feeling material behavior.

The recipe:

- Liquids: pours, sloshes, beads, trickles, splashes. Not "moves."
- Smoke or steam: curls, drifts, billows, thins out. Not "flows," which is too
  liquid a word, and not "moves."
- Fabric or flags: snaps, ripples, luffs, sags. Keep it distinct from the verbs
  used for hair or skin.
- Mechanical parts: clicks into place, ratchets, grinds, whirs up. Distinct from
  organic verbs like "turns" or "swings."

Why: each material class has a real physical behavior with its own descriptive
tradition, fluid dynamics for liquids, aerodynamics for smoke and fabric,
kinematics for mechanisms. Borrowing a generic or human motion verb, "the smoke
moves gracefully," gives the model no material-specific physics to draw on, and
it tends to render the material behaving like a solid object or even a body
instead of the turbulent, non-rigid thing it actually is.

Example: "steam curls off the cup and thins out before it reaches the lens."
Counter-example: "steam moves upward" — a generic verb that produces a uniform,
solid-looking column rising in a straight line rather than a dissipating,
turbulent curl.
