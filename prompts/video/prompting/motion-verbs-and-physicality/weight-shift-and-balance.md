A figure that is standing or sitting still still needs a weight-bearing verb.
"Stands there" or "sits" with nothing else produces a rigid, mannequin pose held
for the whole shot.

The recipe:

- Name where the weight sits: shifts weight onto one hip, leans on the counter,
  rocks back on the heels.
- Give the shift a reason: to reach something, to relieve one leg, to lean in
  and listen more closely.
- Describe transitions between positions as a pivot, sway, or settle, never a
  jump-cut position change.
- For a seated figure, name how the body meets the chair: sinks into, perches on
  the edge of, slouches back into.

Why: humans are never in perfect static balance; small, continuous weight
shifts, what physiologists call postural sway, are exactly what makes a still
figure read as alive rather than posed. Naming one deliberate shift gives the
model a concrete micro-motion to render instead of defaulting to a locked,
symmetrical, statue-still pose held for the shot's full duration.

Example: "she shifts her weight onto one hip and crosses her arms while she
waits."
Counter-example: "she stands there" — produces a rigid, symmetrical, unmoving
pose that reads as posed rather than caught mid-life.
