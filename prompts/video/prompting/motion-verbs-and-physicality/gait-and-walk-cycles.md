Walking breaks into a small set of describable phases (contact, down, passing,
up), and professionals in animation and motion capture reference gait style and
cadence explicitly rather than reaching for "walks naturally."

The recipe:

- Name a real gait style: shuffle, saunter, stomp, limp, stride, totter, swagger.
- Tie the gait to the character's state: a rushed gait shortens stride and raises
  cadence; a tired gait drags the trailing foot and rounds the shoulders.
- Specify what the arms do: a relaxed walk swings arms counter to the legs; a
  tense or hurried walk keeps them tight to the sides.
- Name the surface when it matters: gait changes on gravel, sand, or ice, and
  saying so grounds the shot.

Why: the walk cycle is one of the most heavily studied motions in animation
precisely because small changes in cadence and stride read instantly as
intention and emotional state, before a viewer consciously registers why. "Walks
naturally" hands the model no cadence or stride information to work from, so it
defaults to an even, floaty, unremarkable gait that carries no character.

Example: "she strides fast, arms pumping, heels striking hard on the pavement."
Counter-example: "she walks across the room" — no gait, no pace, no arm
behavior; the result is a flat, generic glide regardless of who the character is
supposed to be.
