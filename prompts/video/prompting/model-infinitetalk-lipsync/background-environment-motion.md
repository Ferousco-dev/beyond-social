A talking-avatar shot is usually a locked or near-locked camera on a subject
whose motion is mostly confined to the face; if the background is also
completely inert, the whole frame reads as a still photo with an animated
mouth composited in, because nothing in the environment corroborates that time
is passing.

- Choose or describe a background with one small, continuous motion source: a
  curtain shifting slightly in a breeze, soft depth-of-field bokeh from
  traffic or foliage behind a window, ambient dust in a light beam.
- Keep background motion subtle and out of focus; anything in sharp focus and
  moving competes with the face for attention and undercuts the shot's
  hierarchy.
- Match the background's motion energy to the subject's energy: a calm,
  measured talking-head read pairs with slow environmental drift, not a busy,
  fast-moving backdrop.
- If the setting is genuinely static (a plain studio backdrop), compensate
  with a very slight camera drift instead, rather than leaving both the
  subject's surroundings and the camera completely locked.

Why: continuous motion somewhere in frame, even out of focus, is what
distinguishes a moment being filmed from a photograph being looked at; a
viewer's peripheral vision registers ambient motion as strongly as it
registers the face, so an inert background undermines the "this is footage"
read regardless of how good the lip-sync itself is.

Example: "soft out-of-focus background with a curtain moving slightly in a
breeze, subject stays sharp, background motion stays subtle and slow."

Counter-example: a flat, static studio backdrop with zero motion and a
perfectly locked camera, the only thing moving anywhere in frame is the mouth,
which is exactly the tell a viewer's eye catches first.
