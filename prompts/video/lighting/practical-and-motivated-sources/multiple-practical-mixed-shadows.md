A room with two or more practicals produces layered, sometimes crossing
shadows: each source casts its own shadow in its own direction, and a subject
can show two shadows at once rather than one clean shadow like a single-key
setup.

- Name each practical's position explicitly (lamp camera-left, window
  camera-right, hallway light behind) and let each cast a shadow consistent
  with its own angle.
- Make the overlap between two shadows the darkest area; where only one
  source reaches, keep that shadow lighter, a partial shadow rather than full
  black.
- Let competing color temperatures compound this: if the two practicals
  differ in color (a warm lamp versus a cool window), the two shadow
  directions can carry a faint hue difference too.
- Don't let a second practical simply neutralize the first one's shadow; it
  should visibly add its own separate shadow, not erase the first.
- Accept that busy, multi-source rooms (a kitchen with overhead, window, and
  range hood light all on) genuinely look a little cluttered or uneven, which
  is correct for that space.

Why: single-shadow, single-direction lighting is the default a studio-style
key light produces, and it's also what generated video defaults to, but any
real room with more than one working fixture produces this layered shadow
signature. Reproducing it is one of the fastest ways to make an interior read
as observed rather than lit for camera.

Example: "warm lamp camera-left and cool window camera-right, subject casting
two faint overlapping shadows in slightly different directions and hues."

Counter-example: "two lamps in frame but the subject has one clean single
shadow." This contradicts having two independent sources, a mismatch a real
lit set would never produce.
