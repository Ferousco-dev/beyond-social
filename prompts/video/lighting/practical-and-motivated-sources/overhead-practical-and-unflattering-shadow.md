A bare or lightly shaded overhead practical (a kitchen pendant, a hallway
bulb, a bathroom ceiling fixture) casts light straight down, producing
specific unflattering shadows, dark eye sockets and a shadow under the nose
and chin, and that unflattering quality is itself the realism cue, not a flaw
to soften.

- Let light fall top-down: brightest on the forehead, cheekbones, and
  shoulders, with shadow pooling in the eye sockets and under the nose, chin,
  and jaw.
- Resist adding a fill light "to see the eyes better"; real overhead-only
  rooms genuinely leave eyes half in shadow.
- Give a bare bulb a harder-edged shadow than a shaded fixture; a paper or
  fabric shade softens the edge somewhat but the top-down direction stays.
- Reserve this for mundane, everyday interior scenes (a hallway, a garage, a
  stairwell, a cheap apartment kitchen) where a single ceiling fixture is
  genuinely the only light source.
- Pair it with light ceiling bounce if the ceiling is light-colored, which can
  slightly soften the harshest shadows, but don't invent a second light to
  compensate.

Why: production lighting almost always avoids a bare overhead as a key
precisely because it's unflattering, so when it shows up unmodified in a shot
it's a strong signal of an unstaged, real domestic space rather than a
considered lighting setup, exactly the cue this product needs for
authenticity over polish.

Example: "single bare overhead bulb in a hallway, shadows pooling under the
eyes and chin, no fill added."

Counter-example: "an overhead bulb in frame but the face is evenly lit with
soft, shadowless skin." The visible fixture and the lighting behavior
contradict each other.
