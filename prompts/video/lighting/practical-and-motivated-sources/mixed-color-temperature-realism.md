Real interiors almost always mix at least two color temperatures at once, most
commonly a warm tungsten practical against cool daylight from a window, and
that clash should read as authentic rather than get smoothed away.

- Use the common real pairing: warm practical light (2700-3000K) in the
  foreground against cooler daylight (5000-6500K) from a window in the
  background.
- Let the shift happen visibly across the subject: the side of the face toward
  a lamp reads warm and orange, the side toward the window reads cooler and
  blue.
- Do not white-balance the whole shot to one neutral temperature; that single
  studio-style correction erases the interior's actual light story.
- Where two practicals of different temperature overlap on the same surface, a
  slightly muddy or greenish transition zone is normal, not a mistake to
  correct.

Why: cameras record color temperature mismatches literally, where human vision
auto-corrects them away, so an unbalanced mix of warm and cool light is a cheap
and reliable signal that a space wasn't staged or corrected for camera. Video
that's uniformly white-balanced across an entire room looks synthetic because
real rooms are almost never lit by one consistent source.

Example: "warm tungsten lamp lighting the subject's left side, cool daylight
spilling in from a window on the right, a visible temperature shift across the
face."

Counter-example: "the entire room rendered in one neutral white balance." Real
interiors are rarely this consistent, and it flattens the sense of a specific,
lived-in space.
