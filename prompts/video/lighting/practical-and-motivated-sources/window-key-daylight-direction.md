A window used as the key light produces natural falloff and softness because it
behaves as one large diffused source close to the subject, and its direction and
color shift with the actual time of day.

- Position the subject 3-6 feet from the window, with the window at roughly
  30-45 degrees off camera axis, not straight-on, which flattens the face.
- North-facing or overcast windows give soft, consistent light all day; a direct
  south-facing sun needs a sheer curtain to diffuse hard shafts.
- Let the far side of the face fall into shadow. Do not ask for even lighting
  across the whole face; the gradient is the point.
- Match color temperature to time of day: warmer (3500-4000K) near sunrise or
  sunset, closer to neutral (5500K) at midday.
- Let the window's soft rectangular light shape show on a nearby wall or the
  floor as visible evidence of the source.

Why: a window this close to the subject is a physically large source, and large
close sources produce a real falloff gradient across a face that a small hard
artificial light cannot fake without extra rigging. A single flat light with no
gradient is one of the fastest tells of generated or CGI-lit video, because it
implies illumination with no actual direction or size.

Example: "subject seated beside a north-facing window, soft light falling off
toward camera-left, sheer curtain diffusing midday sun, far side of the face in
shadow."

Counter-example: "face lit evenly from all sides, no shadow gradient." This
reads like a rendered avatar under ambient light with no physical source behind
it.
