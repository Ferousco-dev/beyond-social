When a lamp is visible in frame, the key light must match its position, height,
color and falloff, or the shot reads as artificially lit despite the prop being
there.

- Set the key light's angle to originate from the lamp's actual position and
  height, usually low (desk or table height), which pushes shadows up under the
  nose and chin rather than down.
- Match color temperature to the bulb type: warm tungsten or halogen sits around
  2700-3000K, warm LED near 2700K, cool LED closer to 4000K.
- Let brightness fall off convincingly close to the lamp; the background stays
  dim rather than getting lit to match the subject.
- Show a visible hotspot or pool of light on the surface directly under the
  shade, not just illumination on the person.
- If the lamp has a shade, note the soft-edged cone it actually casts, not a
  narrow hard beam a bare bulb would produce.

Why: motivated lighting means the visible fixture is doing the real work of
lighting the shot. When the angle, color, or intensity of the actual light
contradicts the prop's position, the lamp becomes decorative rather than
causal, and that mismatch is immediately legible even to a casual viewer who
couldn't say why the shot looks off.

Example: "single desk lamp motivating the key, warm 2800K light pooling on the
desk, shadow falling upward across the ceiling behind the subject."

Counter-example: "lamp visible in frame but face lit evenly from camera
position." The prop is present but not actually lighting anything, an obvious
tell of unmotivated light.
