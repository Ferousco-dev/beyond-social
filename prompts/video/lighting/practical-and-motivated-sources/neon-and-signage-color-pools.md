Neon or LED signage lights a scene in saturated, narrow-band color that pools
and separates by proximity to each tube or letter, rather than washing the
whole frame in a single tint.

- Let each color of tube or letter cast its own pool; don't average colors
  into one gel. A magenta pool and a cyan pool can sit side by side with a
  visible seam between them.
- Keep saturation highest right at the source and fading fast with distance;
  a few feet out it should read as only a faint tint.
- Motivate the direction from wherever the sign actually is in frame, through
  a window or on a wall behind the subject, not from the camera's position.
- Let skin under neon read unnaturally saturated (magenta can push skin toward
  pink or purple); this is correct and shouldn't be neutralized.
- Give reflections on wet pavement, glass, or skin the same hue as the source
  sign they're reflecting.

Why: neon is a narrow-spectrum, physically small source, so real neon-lit
scenes have hard color falloff and competing hues rather than one uniform
wash. A single flat color grade standing in for actual light is the most
common way generated video fakes neon, and it reads as a filter rather than a
place.

Example: "magenta sign light pooling on the camera-left side of the face, cyan
spill from a second sign on the wall behind, color fading to neutral by
mid-frame."

Counter-example: "the whole frame tinted pink to suggest neon." That's a color
grade over otherwise neutral lighting, not light actually falling off from a
source.
