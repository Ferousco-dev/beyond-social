Placing the practical source (a window, a doorway, a bright screen) directly
behind the subject and letting them go partially or fully silhouetted is a
deliberate exposure choice that reads as real camera behavior, not a lighting
mistake.

- Expose for the bright source and let the subject's front fall two or more
  stops under, losing facial detail while keeping a visible rim or edge light
  where the source wraps the silhouette.
- Put the rim light strongest on hair, shoulders, and any edge facing the
  source; the front of the face can go to near-black.
- Add only a thin sliver of fill, from a bounce off the floor or a phone
  screen, to prevent a pure black cutout, which itself reads as graphic and
  illustrated rather than photographic.
- Reserve this for a genuinely bright, large source, like an open doorway at
  midday or an uncovered window; a small, dim practical won't produce a true
  silhouette.
- Hold exposure on the bright source, showing exterior detail and texture,
  rather than blowing it out to pure white.

Why: cameras have limited dynamic range, so a bright background source forces
an underexposed foreground unless a fill light is added. Choosing not to add
that fill and committing to the silhouette is how documentary and narrative
footage actually handles a backlit doorway, and it signals a camera reacting
to real light rather than a renderer flattening dynamic range.

Example: "subject silhouetted in an open doorway, rim light on shoulders and
hair, exterior daylight beyond holding detail, front of the face in shadow."

Counter-example: "a bright doorway behind the subject but their face is fully
lit and detailed." This implies an invisible fill light with no source,
contradicting the exposure the bright doorway would force.
