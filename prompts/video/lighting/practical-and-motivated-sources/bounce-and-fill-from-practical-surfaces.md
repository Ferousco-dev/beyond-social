A practical's light doesn't stop at direct illumination; it bounces off
nearby walls, floors, tabletops, and skin, and that secondary bounce should
tint and softly fill the shadow side rather than leaving it lit by an
unexplained separate source.

- Give bounce the color of the surface it reflects from: a lamp bouncing off
  a warm wood table adds warm fill from below, off a white wall it stays
  close to neutral, off colored fabric or paint it picks up that hue.
- Keep bounce always dimmer and softer than the direct source, a stop or more
  under, with no hard shadow edges of its own.
- Apply the same distance rule as direct light: a surface close to the
  practical bounces usefully, one far away contributes almost nothing.
- Use under-chin and under-nose fill from a tabletop or desk as a common,
  specific bounce cue for anyone seated near a lit table.
- Skip fill entirely in a scene with only one small practical and no
  reflective surfaces nearby; not every shot has bounce, and adding it anyway
  looks like an extra light.

Why: every real surface reflects some light, so treating a practical as the
only phenomenon in the room, direct light then instant black shadow, misses
how interiors actually distribute illumination. Overusing bounce as a blanket
"add soft fill" ignores that it only exists where a reflective surface and
proximity actually justify it.

Example: "lamp light bouncing warm off the wooden desk, soft under-chin fill
visible, shadow side of the face still clearly darker than the lit side."

Counter-example: "the shadow side of the face is fully lit despite only one
small lamp in frame." This implies a second, unexplained fill light.
