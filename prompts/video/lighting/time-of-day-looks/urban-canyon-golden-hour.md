In a dense city, tall buildings cut golden hour's usable window short and reshape
it into narrow shafts and bounced color rather than the open, even wash available
in unobstructed landscapes. This is a genuinely different look from suburban or
rural golden hour, not just a backdrop swap.

The recipe:

- Let direct sun only hit a scene when it can travel down a street's exact
  alignment or over a lower rooftop line; most of a street falls into building
  shadow well before actual sunset.
- Where direct light does reach, make it a hard, narrow shaft crossing the
  frame between buildings, not a broad wash, catching dust, steam from vents, or
  pedestrians as it goes.
- Let warm sun bouncing off brick, sandstone, or glass curtain walls tint the
  shadowed areas around it; an orange building face can throw warm bounce onto a
  subject standing in open shade nearby.
- Allow glass towers to throw a secondary, delayed "reflected golden hour" onto
  streets already in shadow, sometimes minutes after direct light left ground
  level.
- Mix in practical city lights, traffic signals, shop signs, interior office
  lights, already on by the time golden hour hits street level, since the canyon
  effect shortens the day early.

Why: skyscraper height relative to street width determines how much direct sun
any given ground-level point actually receives, so the "golden hour" experienced
at street level in a real city is a fragmented, reflected, shortened version of
the open-sky one, and prompting for the open version in a dense urban setting
produces a physically implausible result.

Example: "narrow shaft of golden light cutting between two buildings, steam
rising from a vent catching the light, rest of the street already in blue
shadow, shop signs on."

Counter-example: an open, even golden-hour wash filling an entire dense downtown
street from sidewalk to sidewalk. Buildings that tall would already be blocking
that light at that sun angle.
