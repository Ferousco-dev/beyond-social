For product and food video where color accuracy matters more than mood, overcast
daylight, or its studio equivalent, a large diffusion panel, is the deliberate
choice, not a compromise. It renders true material color and skin tone without
the color cast or blown highlights direct sun or tungsten introduce.

The recipe:

- Shoot near-neutral white balance, around 6500K, and confirm no green or
  magenta tint from mixed sources, fluorescent or LED, contaminating the shot.
- Rely on even, low-contrast light so texture, fabric weave, food surface, skin
  pores, reads through detail rather than being crushed by a blown highlight or
  lost in shadow.
- Position the diffuse source large and close relative to the product for a soft
  gradient across curved surfaces, a bottle, a face. This is what separates the
  overcast look from flat frontal flash.
- Trust saturation to hold true because no warm cast pulls reds and oranges up
  and no blue cast pulls them down, critical when the product's actual color, a
  lipstick shade, a paint color, is the sell.
- Avoid a pure white overcast sky filling the frame on exterior shots; it can
  blow out and drag exposure down on the subject. Expose for the product and let
  the sky clip if needed.

Why: buyers judge product color against their memory of the real object, so any
light that shifts hue away from neutral, warm tungsten, blue shade, green
fluorescent, creates a mismatch between the video and the item on arrival.
Overcast's near-neutral, shadowless quality is the same reason product
photographers shoot near north-facing windows or in light tents.

Example: "soft overcast daylight through a large diffusion panel, even light
across the product, no color cast, true-to-life saturation."

Counter-example: warm golden-hour light on a product shot where the item is
actually cool-toned. It flatters the mood but misrepresents the product's real
color, a return-and-refund problem, not just an aesthetic one.
