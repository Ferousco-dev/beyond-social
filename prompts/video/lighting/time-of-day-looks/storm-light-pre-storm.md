The light in the 20-40 minutes before a storm arrives, sun still partially
breaking through gaps in an advancing dark cloud front, produces an unusually
high-contrast, sometimes greenish-yellow ambient combined with a darkening sky,
distinct from both ordinary overcast and clear-day light.

The recipe:

- Invert sky brightness: the sky can be darker than the sunlit ground, since the
  storm cell blocks the sun from above while direct light still rakes in from a
  clear gap at the horizon.
- Let a localized hard sun break through cloud gaps to create isolated hot spots
  on the landscape against an otherwise dark, moody sky, not uniform lighting.
- Sell it with wind-driven cues: bending trees, blowing debris, hair and
  clothing moving, dust or first rain at frame edges.
- Add a subtle greenish or sickly-yellow cast in the shadows, from light
  filtering through a thick, moisture-laden cloud base, as a slight desaturated
  shift, not a heavy color filter over everything.
- Specify sun position relative to the storm front, since sun behind camera
  lighting the storm face and storm behind camera with sun still ahead look very
  different, and the window is short.

Why: the effect comes from two competing light sources at once, direct sunlight
from a clear gap and the deep shadow of an advancing cloud mass, and that
competition, plus the physical drop in light a photographer's eye recognizes as
"storm's coming," is what makes it read as a specific, dramatic, time-limited
moment rather than generic bad weather.

Example: "dark storm clouds rolling in behind, a shaft of direct hard sun still
lighting the foreground, wind bending the grass, slight greenish cast in the
shadows."

Counter-example: uniformly dark, flat grey "storm" lighting with no sun break
and no wind motion. It reads as ordinary overcast at a darker exposure, not an
approaching storm.
