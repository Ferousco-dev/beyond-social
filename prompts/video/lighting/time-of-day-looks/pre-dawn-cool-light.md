The 30-45 minutes before sunrise produce a distinct, colder, dimmer light than
post-sunset blue hour: the same blue-dominant palette, but paired with near-total
human absence, condensation, and a rising rather than falling light curve.

The recipe:

- Keep light dimmer and more desaturated than post-sunset blue hour at an
  equivalent sun elevation; the world hasn't been warmed by a full day of sun
  yet, so surfaces and skin read colder and flatter.
- Include physical cold-world cues: visible breath in cool climates, dew or
  frost on surfaces, closed shutters, parked cars with condensation on the
  windshield.
- Make light increase shot to shot, not decrease: each cut should read
  marginally brighter than the last, the opposite of a dusk sequence.
- Lean on near-total absence of people and traffic as the strongest available
  cue; a single figure, a runner, a delivery worker, reads as intentional and
  specific rather than generic.
- Hold color temp high, roughly 9000-11000K, with almost no warm practicals lit
  yet except isolated ones, a porch light, a 24-hour sign.

Why: since the sun hasn't begun heating the ground and no daytime warmth has
accumulated anywhere in the scene, pre-dawn light lacks the residual warmth that
lingers into post-sunset blue hour from the day just ended. That absence, plus
the emptiness of a world that hasn't started its day, separates the two even
when the raw color-temperature numbers are similar.

Example: "pre-dawn street, empty of people, condensation on parked car windows,
cold blue-grey light, one porch light still on."

Counter-example: pre-dawn light applied to a busy, warm-toned street with
visible daytime activity. The human context contradicts the hour regardless of
correct color grading.
