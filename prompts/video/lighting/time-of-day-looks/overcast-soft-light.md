A fully overcast sky turns the entire cloud layer into one enormous diffuser,
producing soft, nearly shadowless light with a cool-neutral color temperature and
even saturation across the frame: the same physics as a softbox, just much larger.

The recipe:

- Shadows are soft-edged and low-contrast, sometimes barely visible on the ground,
  never a hard cutoff.
- Color temp runs 6500-7500K, slightly cooler than clear daylight, which can mute
  warm skin tones if not corrected.
- Saturation in foliage and painted surfaces reads higher because no blown
  highlight competes with the color, even though overall contrast is lower.
- No visible sun disc, no lens flare, and a single soft, large catchlight in the
  eyes instead of a small hard sun-point.
- The sky itself often reads flat white or grey and can blow out; favor exposing
  for the ground subject over the sky.

Why: clouds scatter direct sunlight in every direction before it reaches the
ground, so instead of one small hard source, the entire sky becomes the source.
A source that large relative to the subject wraps light around edges and
eliminates hard shadow, the same principle that makes a large softbox flattering
up close.

Example: "flat overcast sky, soft even light with no visible shadow direction,
muted cool-neutral color, single soft catchlight in the eyes."

Counter-example: "overcast" light that still shows a hard directional shadow and
a visible sun disc. That contradicts the premise; it is clear sky with light
haze, not true overcast.
