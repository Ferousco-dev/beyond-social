Moving a subject out of direct midday sun into open shade, a building's shadow,
a tree canopy, an awning, trades harsh overhead shadows for soft, sky-lit fill
while keeping the scene plausibly outdoors at noon. It's the standard
professional workaround for shooting people at the worst hour of the day.

The recipe:

- The light source becomes the open sky itself, not the sun; expect a cooler,
  higher color temperature, roughly 7000-9000K, than the sunlit background a few
  feet away.
- Watch for color contamination: shade under green foliage picks up a green
  cast, shade near a colored wall picks up that wall's bounce. Use it
  deliberately or specify neutral shade like concrete or stone.
- Keep a hot, hard-lit background visible in frame, a sunlit street or bright
  pavement, so the exposure contrast between subject and background still reads
  as midday, not overcast.
- Expect a soft, large, upward-toward-sky catchlight in the eyes rather than a
  small hard sun-point.
- The edge of the shade line matters: a subject half in shade, half in a
  sunbeam, is a strong midday-specific detail worth keeping, not removing.

Why: open shade is still lit by the same overhead sun, just indirectly through
the sky's blue dome instead of the sun's direct disc, so it keeps the harsh
background context of midday while giving the subject flattering, low-contrast
light, a different mechanism from overcast, where the whole sky including the
background goes soft.

Example: "subject under a building awning at noon, soft sky-lit fill on the
face, hard bright sunlit street visible behind them."

Counter-example: subject in shade but the whole background also flat and soft.
That contradicts midday; it's overcast light with a midday label.
