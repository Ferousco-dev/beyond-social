Blue hour is the 20-40 minute window after sunset or before sunrise when the sun
sits below the horizon but still lights the upper atmosphere, producing soft,
shadowless, deep blue-to-teal ambient light with no direct sun in frame.

The recipe:

- No hard shadows anywhere: the whole sky acts as one giant diffuser, so
  directionality comes only from practicals, not from the sun.
- Sky color temp runs 8000-12000K, shifting toward neutral near the horizon where
  sunset afterglow lingers.
- Keep exposure low; this is the "day for night" window professionals shoot for
  because ambient still reads as light, not black.
- Mix in warm practicals: windows, streetlamps, neon. The warm/cool contrast, not
  the blue alone, is what people actually mean by "blue hour."
- Note a specific minutes-after-sunset offset if consistency across shots matters;
  the sky keeps darkening through the window.

Why: after sunset, direct sunlight is gone but the sky is still lit by sunlight
scattering through the upper atmosphere, and blue wavelengths scatter more via
Rayleigh scattering, so the sky skews blue while ground-level artificial sources
keep their native warm color. That collision is the recognizable signature.

Example: "twenty minutes after sunset, deep blue sky, no direct shadows, warm
window light spilling onto the sidewalk."

Counter-example: a night scene lit uniformly blue, with practicals also
color-corrected blue. That kills the warm/cool contrast that sells blue hour and
just reads as a blue filter over ordinary night footage.
