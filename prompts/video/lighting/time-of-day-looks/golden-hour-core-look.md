Golden hour, the roughly hour-long window after sunrise or before sunset, is defined
by a sun angle under about 10 degrees that puts light nearly parallel to the ground,
producing long shadows and a warm 2800-3400K color temperature that reads as
flattering because it suppresses the blue wavelengths that expose skin imperfections.

The recipe:

- Shadows stretch 3-6x subject height and fall nearly horizontal across the frame,
  not pooled underneath.
- Color temp 2800-3400K: skin reads warm and amber, not the neutral 5600K of midday.
- Contrast is lower than midday because atmosphere scatters out blue light, but
  shadows stay directional, never flat.
- Specify light direction relative to camera: sun behind camera (flat warm wash),
  sun to the side (modeling across the face), or sun behind subject (a separate,
  backlit setup).
- Add visible haze or dust motes catching the beam; real golden hour shows
  atmosphere, not just a color grade.

Why: at a low sun angle, light travels through far more atmosphere than at noon,
and that longer path scatters short (blue) wavelengths out of the direct beam via
Rayleigh scattering, leaving the warm, long wavelengths dominant. The low angle also
casts shadows horizontally across a scene instead of pooling under objects, which is
the geometric signature that separates real golden hour from a warm color grade
applied to midday footage.

Example: "low sun at camera-left, long shadows raking across the pavement, 3200K
warm wash on skin, faint dust visible in the light shafts."

Counter-example: a warm color grade laid over flat midday lighting with shadows
pooling straight down. The color says golden hour but the shadow geometry says
noon, and the contradiction reads as an "orange filter," not real light.
