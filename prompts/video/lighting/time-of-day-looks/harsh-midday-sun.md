Sun near solar noon, roughly 60-90 degrees altitude, produces small, hard-edged
shadows that fall almost straight down, high contrast with no natural fill, and
specular highlights. Most productions avoid it for beauty work, but it reads as
urgent and authentic for documentary, sports, and UGC content.

The recipe:

- Shadows pool directly under subjects and features (under eyes, nose, chin),
  never stretched across the frame.
- Contrast ratio commonly 8:1 or higher between highlight and shadow, with
  highlights able to clip while shadow detail holds little information.
- Color temp near neutral daylight, 5500-6500K, no warm cast.
- Include physical responses: squinting, sweat, glare, sunglasses reflections.
  These are period-accurate reactions to real overhead sun.
- Use it as a narrative cue deliberately: "high noon" light signals exposure,
  heat, or raised stakes, not a default setting.

Why: at solar noon the sun's rays travel through the least atmosphere and stay
tightly collimated, so the shadow edge stays sharp and the sun behaves like a
small, hard key rather than a large wrapped source. That is exactly why beauty
lighting avoids it, and exactly why it reads as unlit, real-world footage instead
of a produced shot.

Example: "sun directly overhead, hard shadow pooling under the cap brim, subject
squinting, sweat visible, high contrast on the pavement."

Counter-example: "overhead sun" softened into wraparound light with no visible
shadow. That is a softbox pretending to be the sun, and the mismatch between a
noon setting and soft shadows reads as staged.
