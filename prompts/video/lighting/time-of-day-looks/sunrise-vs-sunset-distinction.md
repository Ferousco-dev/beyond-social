Sunrise and sunset share the same low sun angle and a similar color-temperature
range, but they are not interchangeable in a shot. The air itself, the human
activity in frame, and the direction the light is moving all differ, and getting
these wrong is a common tell that the scene wasn't observed, only labeled.

The recipe:

- Air clarity: mornings are typically cooler with less accumulated haze, so
  sunrise light often reads crisper; evenings carry more haze and humidity,
  scattering light into a softer, sometimes redder glow.
- Activity cue: sunrise plausibly shows near-empty streets, joggers, delivery
  trucks, dew on surfaces; sunset plausibly shows dispersing crowds, traffic,
  patio activity, long shadows crossing already-active spaces.
- Light direction over time: sunrise light is rising and still warming; sunset
  light is falling and about to vanish. Match the movement to the moment.
- Keep shadow orientation tied to real compass direction: east-facing subjects
  lit at sunrise, west-facing at sunset, and don't reverse the setup between
  paired shots meant to be the same scene.

Why: a viewer reads context (activity, air quality, dew, crowd state) faster
than color temperature, so a technically correct warm, low-angle shot still
feels wrong if the surrounding world doesn't match the hour it claims.

Example: "sunrise, empty street, dew on parked cars, a single jogger, cool clean
air, sun still climbing."

Counter-example: labeling a shot "sunrise" while showing a packed happy-hour
patio in hazy dusk light. The scene content contradicts the time of day
regardless of a correct sun angle.
