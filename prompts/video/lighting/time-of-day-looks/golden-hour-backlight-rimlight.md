Putting the low golden-hour sun behind the subject turns it into a rim or hair
light that separates the subject from the background with a bright edge, while
the front of the subject falls into shadow. This is the most-requested
"cinematic" golden-hour setup, and it fails without a specific fill strategy.

The recipe:

- Position the sun 15-45 degrees off-axis behind the subject; dead-center behind
  causes a total silhouette unless that's the intent.
- Expect a visible rim along hair, shoulders, and fabric edges; skin facing
  camera goes 1-2 stops darker without fill.
- Add a plausible fill source: bounce off a light-colored wall, sand, or
  pavement, or a nearby white shirt. Unmotivated frontal brightness reads as
  artificial.
- Allow real lens behavior: veiling flare that slightly washes contrast, a soft
  flare streak if the sun edges into frame, faint halation around the brightest
  edge. These optical imperfections are what sell a camera, not a render.
- Keep the sun's disc or its brightest sliver near the frame edge or partially
  occluded by the subject; a fully round, centered sun disc reads as pasted in.

Why: backlight does a separation job depth of field alone can't at a distance,
but it only reads as real when the falloff on the front of the subject and the
flare artifacts match what a lens actually does with a bright source that close
to frame.

Example: "sun low behind the subject's right shoulder, rim light on hair and
jacket edge, soft bounce fill from light pavement below, faint flare where the
sun edge grazes frame."

Counter-example: even, bright frontal exposure with a golden rim added on top.
No falloff, no flare, no shadow logic; it reads as an effect layered over flat
footage.
