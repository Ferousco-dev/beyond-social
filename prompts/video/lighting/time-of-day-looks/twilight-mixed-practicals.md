In the 30-60 minutes as blue hour deepens into night, the defining look is not
blue alone but the collision of a fading cool sky with warming artificial
sources: streetlights, shopfronts, headlights, neon, all switching on at
different times and different color temperatures.

The recipe:

- Layer at least two artificial color temperatures: sodium-vapor streetlight
  (roughly 2200K, amber-orange), LED shopfronts (roughly 4000-6500K, cooler
  white), warm incandescent interiors (roughly 2700K). Real streets rarely
  carry one uniform artificial color.
- Let the sky ambient keep dropping in brightness across the shot rather than
  holding static; twilight is a moving target, not a fixed state.
- Keep some lights not yet on: a dark storefront, an untriggered streetlight, a
  truck with its headlights still off. Patchiness reads as real; uniform
  illumination reads as a rig.
- Give wet pavement or glass visible color-mixed reflections, streaks of amber
  and cool white together, a strong "real dusk street" cue.
- Let faces lit by mixed sources show mixed color on skin, one cheek warmer from
  a shopfront, the other cooler from residual sky, not one flat white balance.

Why: real twilight scenes are lit by many independent, un-synchronized sources
running on different bulb technologies, so the color mixing is inherently
uneven, and that unevenness is what a single-source studio "dusk" fails to
reproduce.

Example: "dusk street, sodium streetlights just switching on, cool blue sky
fading above, wet asphalt reflecting mixed amber and white light."

Counter-example: a dusk scene where every source and every reflection carries
the identical cool-blue grade. It looks like a color filter over day footage,
not multiple real bulbs.
