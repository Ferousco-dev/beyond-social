Once a key angle is set, whether it lights the side of the face turned toward
camera or away from camera changes how wide or narrow the face reads, a
distinction independent of the key's up-down angle.

- Short lighting: the key lights the side of the face turned away from
  camera, so the near side (turned toward camera) falls into shadow. This
  narrows and slims the face and is the more common, more flattering choice
  for a three-quarter turned subject.
- Broad lighting: the key lights the side of the face turned toward camera,
  so the near side is bright and the far side falls into shadow. This widens
  the face and reads as more direct, less sculpted, occasionally used for a
  flatter, more confrontational look.
- The subject's head angle relative to camera has to be stated too — this
  distinction only exists when the face is turned at least slightly off
  camera-axis; a straight-on face makes short vs. broad moot.
- Combine with a stated key angle: "short lighting, key on the far side, head
  turned 20 degrees off camera" is a complete, unambiguous instruction.

Why: face width is one of the more noticeable things viewers register
subconsciously in a talking-head shot, and this single choice, layered on top
of key angle, is what studio photographers actually adjust to control it.

Example: "subject's head turned slightly camera-right, short lighting, key
illuminating the far cheek, near cheek in soft shadow."
Counter-example: "flattering lighting on the face" — gives no head-turn and no
near/far side instruction, so the model cannot apply this distinction at all.
