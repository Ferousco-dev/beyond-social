A strong backlight or rim source pointed roughly toward camera should
interact with the lens, not just the subject — real cameras bloom, flare, and
lose a little contrast when a bright source sits near the frame edge or just
out of frame, and naming that is what sells the light as physically present
rather than composited on top of the shot.

- Ask for a touch of lens flare or veiling glare only when the rim source
  would plausibly be near or within the frame (a low sun, a window just out of
  shot, a streetlamp) — an off-axis rim behind a wall has no reason to flare.
- Describe bloom around the rim's brightest edge (highlights spreading
  slightly into surrounding dark areas) rather than a sharp, contained line of
  light — real optics do not produce perfectly crisp bright edges against dark
  backgrounds.
- Keep flare subtle and directional (consistent with the stated source
  position), not a generic scattered sparkle effect across the whole frame.
- Skip this entirely for a rim source behind the subject and out of the
  lens's direct line of sight — flare only belongs when the geometry
  justifies it.

Why: a perfectly crisp, non-interacting rim highlight is optically implausible
whenever the source is anywhere near the lens axis, and viewers register that
missing imperfection, even without consciously naming it, as the shot having
been assembled rather than actually filmed.

Example: "low sun just out of frame behind the subject, warm rim light on
their shoulder and hair, a touch of lens flare and glare where the sun nearly
clips the frame edge."
Counter-example: a bright rim source clearly near the lens axis rendered with
a perfectly clean, flare-free edge — physically inconsistent with how real
lenses handle near-axis backlight.
