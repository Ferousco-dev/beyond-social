Real interiors almost never have one consistent light color: daylight through
a window, a tungsten lamp, and a screen glow all sit at different color
temperatures at once. Giving key, fill, and rim different, sourced color
temperatures is one of the fastest realism upgrades available.

- Key from daylight (window, overcast sky): cool, slightly blue-white.
- Fill from room bounce or a tungsten lamp: warmer, amber-leaning.
- Rim from a different source again (another window, a neon sign, a screen):
  its own distinct color, often the most saturated of the three.
- State the mismatch explicitly and tie each color to its source: "cool
  daylight key, warm lamp fill, magenta neon rim" — not just "colorful
  lighting."
- Reserve a fully matched, neutral white-balanced rig (all three lights the
  same temperature) for clean product or corporate work, where color neutrality
  is the goal, not a flaw to fix.

Why: a camera or the human eye white-balances to one dominant source, which is
exactly why the other sources in frame read as visibly warm or cool rather
than neutral; a rig where every light is the same clean white is a tell that
it was lit by a single artificial process rather than a real room with
multiple light sources at different temperatures.

Example: "cool blue-white key from a window, warm amber fill bouncing off a
wood table, magenta rim from a neon sign visible in the background."
Counter-example: "well-balanced, neutral white lighting" applied to a scene
with a lamp and a window both in frame — physically inconsistent with the
visible sources and reads as artificially color-corrected.
