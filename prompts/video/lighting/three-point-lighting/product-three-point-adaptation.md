Three-point lighting on an object instead of a face shifts the goal from
modeling a face to controlling specular highlights and edge definition on
whatever the product's surface is doing — glass, matte plastic, metal, fabric
all behave differently under the same rig.

- Key becomes the light that defines the product's main form: a large soft
  source close to the product for a smooth, wrapping highlight on rounded or
  glossy items; a smaller harder source for crisp reflections on flat glass or
  metal faces.
- Fill controls how deep the shadow side of the product goes — critical on
  dark or black products, where zero fill loses all surface detail into pure
  black.
- Rim (often called a kicker or edge light on product sets) traces the
  silhouette against the background, essential when the product and
  background are close in value or color.
- Name what the highlight should do on the specific material: "a long soft
  highlight streak along the bottle's curve" for glass, versus "a tight hot
  spot" for brushed metal — material dictates highlight shape.

Why: an object has no face-perception shortcuts to rely on, so the viewer
reads quality entirely from how convincingly light interacts with its
material, meaning generic "well lit product" language produces flat renders
that fail to sell texture or premium-ness.

Example: "soft key light close to the bottle producing a long smooth highlight
along its curve, dark background, thin white kicker light tracing the right
edge to separate it from the shadow."
Counter-example: "product shot, professional lighting, clean background" —
material, highlight shape, and separation are all left unspecified and
default to a flat, generic render.
