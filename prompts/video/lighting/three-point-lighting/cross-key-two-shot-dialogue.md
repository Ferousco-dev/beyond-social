Two people facing each other in a two-shot need their own key lights aimed
across the frame at each other, not one shared front light — otherwise both
faces flatten identically and the shot loses the sense of two separate people
occupying real space.

- Each person's key comes from the side nearer the other person, angled
  across so it also functions as loose backlight/rim for their scene partner
  — this is the classic cross-key setup used in sitcom and dialogue coverage.
- State each subject's key separately: "key on subject A from camera-right,
  key on subject B from camera-left" rather than one lighting instruction
  covering both.
- Keep a consistent overall color temperature and quality (both soft, or both
  matching hardness) across both keys unless the scene specifically wants an
  imbalance (one person more lit than the other to signal status or mood).
  Mismatched randomness reads as an error, not a choice.
- For over-the-shoulder or single coverage of the same scene, keep each
  person's key description consistent shot to shot — this is a continuity
  requirement, not just an initial lighting choice.

Why: a single flat front light on two facing subjects removes exactly the
directional shadow cues that let a viewer read spatial depth and separation
between them, so cross-key is what makes a two-shot look staged in a real
room rather than pasted together.

Example: "two subjects facing each other, key on the left subject from
camera-right, key on the right subject from camera-left, both soft sources,
matching warm color temperature."
Counter-example: "both subjects well lit" as one instruction — collapses into
one shared flat front light with no cross-directionality between them.
