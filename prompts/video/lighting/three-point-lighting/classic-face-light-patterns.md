Small changes in key light height and angle produce named, recognizable
shadow patterns on a face. Naming the pattern is a shortcut to a precise,
photographically correct key placement instead of a vague angle description.

- Loop lighting: key slightly above eye level and about 30-45 degrees off
  axis, casting a small shadow of the nose that loops down toward the corner
  of the mouth without touching the cheek shadow. The standard, flattering
  default for most faces.
- Rembrandt lighting: key higher and further around, producing a small
  triangle of light on the shadow-side cheek, bounded by the nose shadow and
  cheek shadow meeting. Moodier, more painterly, associated with drama and
  gravitas.
- Butterfly (paramount) lighting: key directly above and in front, camera
  axis, casting a small symmetrical shadow directly under the nose. Glamour
  and beauty convention, very flattering, slightly formal.
- Split lighting: key at 90 degrees to the side, lighting exactly half the
  face. High drama, used sparingly, easy to overdo.

Why: these patterns are named because they are stable, testable outcomes of a
specific key position, so requesting one by name gives the model a precise
geometric target instead of an ambiguous verbal angle.

Example: "loop lighting on the subject's face, key slightly above eye level,
soft nose shadow looping toward the mouth."
Counter-example: "dramatic lighting on the face" with no named pattern — too
ambiguous to reliably produce a specific, repeatable shadow shape.
