Real interiors rarely run on one color temperature; naming the clash between
sources is a realism cue that outdoor scenes, lit by one dominant sun or sky,
almost never need.

- Tungsten lamps (roughly 2700K, orange-warm) and daylight through a window
  (roughly 5600K, blue-cool) commonly share a frame. Name at least two sources
  and let them visibly disagree in color.
- Decide which source the camera is "white-balanced" to: pick one to read neutral
  and let the others carry a visible cast, an orange lamp glow in an otherwise
  blue-lit room, or a warm room with a cool blue window-shaped patch of light.
- Uncorrected mixed light should divide the frame into distinct color zones, not
  blend into one averaged hue.
- Overhead fluorescent or cheap LED fixtures often sit slightly green-white
  (roughly 4000-5000K with a tint); naming that tint against a warm lamp sells a
  real office or kitchen instantly.

Why: outdoor scenes look color-consistent by default because they have one
dominant source; interiors with a single uniform color temperature read as
artificial because real rooms almost never have matched fixtures, so the visible
clash is evidence of an unstaged, real space rather than a color-graded set.

Example: "warm tungsten lamp lighting the desk, cool blue daylight spilling in
from the window behind, the two colors visibly clashing across the room."
Counter-example: "warm, cozy lighting throughout the room" — one uniform hue with
no second named source, reading as a LUT applied over the whole frame.
