Visible light beams behave differently indoors, a narrow shaft with dust motes,
than outdoors, broad atmospheric haze, and conflating the two is a common tell.

- Indoors, a single shaft of window or door light through a dim room reveals fine
  dust motes drifting inside the beam. Name "visible dust motes floating in the
  light shaft" for a narrow, contained, high-contrast beam against a darker room.
- Outdoors, atmosphere (light haze, humidity, mist, or smoke) scatters light
  across the whole scene, not a narrow shaft. Rays through trees or buildings are
  broad, soft-edged, and fade gradually rather than staying a crisp beam.
- Indoor beams need a dark surrounding room to read at all, contrast is what
  makes the shaft visible; outdoor haze reads even in open daylight because it
  scatters across the whole frame, not just inside one shaft.
- Don't combine both cues in one description; a "dusty light shaft" outdoors or
  "hazy atmosphere" filling an entire small room both misread the physics of
  their space.

Why: this level of specificity keeps volumetric light physically anchored to its
environment instead of applying a generic "light rays" effect that looks
identical regardless of setting, a common and easily spotted AI shortcut.

Example: "narrow shaft of window light cuts across the dim room, fine dust
visible drifting inside the beam."
Counter-example: "dusty rays of light" in an open outdoor field with no enclosing
dark space to give the shaft contrast, physically incoherent.
