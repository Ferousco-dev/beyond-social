After dark, indoor and outdoor light diverge in color and brightness in a way
that's the opposite of their daytime relationship; naming which one you're in
avoids a muddy, averaged "night" look.

- Outdoor night light is a patchwork of small, colored point sources at a
  distance: sodium-vapor streetlights (amber-orange), LED signage or storefronts
  (cool white or mixed color), neon (saturated color), all against a near-black
  sky. Name the specific fixtures rather than "street lighting."
- Indoor night light comes from close, warm practicals, lamps, overhead
  fixtures, a TV or phone screen, and becomes the dominant source in frame
  precisely because there's no daylight competing with it; interiors read
  comparatively bright and warm against a dark exterior seen through a window.
- A night exterior with a lit interior visible through windows should show the
  interior as small warm rectangles punched into the dark scene, not lit up to
  full interior brightness across the whole frame.
- Skin under night streetlight often carries a visible amber or sodium cast
  unless corrected; naming "unflattering sodium streetlight cast on skin" versus
  "clean white LED signage light" changes the read considerably.

Why: daytime outdoor light is the brighter, dominant source with indoor light
comparatively dim, but at night that relationship flips, and prompts that don't
account for the flip tend to render night exteriors as evenly, implausibly
bright.

Example: "night street lit by scattered sodium streetlights, a shop window
glowing warm and bright against the dark sidewalk, cool neon sign reflected on
wet pavement."
Counter-example: "nighttime, well lit street" with no named sources, defaulting
to a flat, evenly bright night that ignores how dark real night exteriors are
between pools of light.
