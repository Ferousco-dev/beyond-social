A shot that moves a subject through a doorway or window plane, indoor to outdoor
or back, has to carry the lighting change with the motion, not skip it at a cut.

- As the subject crosses the threshold, state the light shift explicitly across
  the motion: "walks from the dim tungsten-lit hallway into bright open-shade
  daylight at the door, skin tone shifting from warm to neutral as they cross."
- Keep the transition point at the doorway itself; light should change roughly
  where the physical boundary is, not gradually across the whole room or
  instantly at a cut.
- A brief silhouette is common and correct at the threshold: the subject rims
  dark against the brighter exterior (or interior, at night) for a beat before
  exposure resolves on their face. Naming that beat sells the physical
  transition.
- If the camera holds still at the threshold rather than moving through it, split
  the frame's exposure logic instead: interior side dimmer and warmer, exterior
  side brighter and cooler, meeting at the doorframe.

Why: this is a compound case of exposure balance and color-temperature contrast
happening within one continuous motion, and it's exactly the kind of shot where an
unspecified prompt tends to average the two lighting conditions into one flat,
physically impossible hallway-brightness value for the whole move.

Example: "steps from a warm dim interior hallway through the front doorway into
bright cool daylight, briefly silhouetted in the doorframe before the exterior
light resolves on their face."
Counter-example: "walks outside, evenly lit the whole time" — erases the exposure
and color jump that makes the threshold moment read as real.
