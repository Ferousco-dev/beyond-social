Shooting toward a bright window forces an exposure choice between the window and
the interior; naming which one is correctly exposed is a small, high-value cue.

- Expose for the interior and the window blows out to a bright, textureless white
  rectangle. Describe it as "window blown out to pure white behind the subject"
  if that's the intended, common look.
- Expose for the window and the interior underexposes toward silhouette:
  "subject silhouetted against a properly exposed window, interior detail lost in
  shadow," a classic moody doorway shot.
- A balanced look, where both window and interior hold detail, implies added
  fill light or a neutral-density gel on the glass in a real shoot. Name it if you
  want it: "window gelled down to match interior exposure, daylight visible
  through glass with detail."
- State the choice; leaving it unstated lets the model default to an evenly
  exposed everything, erasing the specific interior-versus-exterior contrast that
  sells a real room.

Why: real cameras have limited dynamic range, so a shooter is always forced to
pick which side of the glass keeps detail, and naming that tradeoff is exactly the
kind of physically-motivated limitation a sourceless AI render tends to skip by
making both sides impossibly, evenly visible.

Example: "interior exposed correctly, the window behind blown out to flat white,
no visible exterior detail."
Counter-example: "bright room with a clear view out the window and everyone well
lit" — flattens the real exposure tradeoff into an impossible, evenly-lit HDR
look.
