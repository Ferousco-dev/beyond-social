Indoor light is shaped by the room itself: low ceilings and colored walls bounce
and tint it in ways an open outdoor scene, with no enclosing surfaces, never does.

- A white ceiling under roughly 2.5-3m bounces key light back down softly, filling
  under-eye and chin shadow. Name it: "light bounces off the low white ceiling,
  softening the shadows under the eyes."
- Colored or wood-paneled walls tint the bounce. A red-brick wall throws a subtle
  warm-red kick onto the near side of a subject; name the wall material when it
  isn't neutral.
- Corners and areas away from windows or fixtures go dark faster indoors than
  outdoors, since there's no sky dome to fill them. Call out "the far corner
  falls into shadow" for a single-source room.
- Small rooms compress the effective source size, the whole room becomes part of
  the softbox, so a tight interior often looks softer than a same-distance window
  shot in a large room.

Why: outdoor bounce comes from the sky and ground and is nearly colorless; indoor
bounce comes from finite, often-colored surfaces close to the subject, so naming
the material and proximity of ceiling and walls is what separates a real,
enclosed room from a sourceless void with a subject floating in it.

Example: "low white ceiling bounces the lamp light back down, warm timber floor
kicks a faint amber glow up onto the jaw."
Counter-example: "evenly lit room" with no ceiling, wall, or floor bounce named,
producing the flat, room-less look of a subject composited onto a green screen.
