Glass and mirrors indoors and outdoors reflect different content, and naming what
they show is a targeted, cheap realism cue.

- An outdoor storefront window or car windshield reflects sky, street, and
  passing exterior elements. Name it: "shop window reflecting the overcast sky
  and blurred street traffic behind the subject."
- An indoor mirror or dark television screen reflects the room itself, other
  furniture, a lamp's glow, the subject's own back: "dark TV screen faintly
  reflecting the lamp-lit room behind camera."
- Glass indoors near a bright window can partially mirror the interior while also
  showing exterior through it, a layered look, especially near dusk when interior
  and exterior brightness are close: "window glass showing both the dim interior
  reflection and the darkening street outside."
- Match reflection content to the stated environment; a reflection showing the
  wrong scene undercuts an otherwise well-lit shot.

Why: reflections are effectively a second, smaller shot embedded in the frame, so
what they show has to obey the same environment logic as the primary scene, and
naming it stops the model from painting a generic, contextless reflection or
skipping the reflective surface's content entirely.

Example: "storefront glass reflecting the grey sky and a passing bus, subject
visible through the glass beyond."
Counter-example: an indoor mirror described as reflecting "a nice view" with no
relation to the stated room, an ungrounded reflection that breaks the scene's
internal logic.
