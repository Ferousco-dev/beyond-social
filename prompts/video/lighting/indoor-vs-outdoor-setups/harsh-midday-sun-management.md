Overhead midday sun is the hardest outdoor light to shoot flattering faces in, and
naming the fix matters as much as naming the problem.

- Direct overhead sun, roughly 60-90 degrees elevation, casts hard shadows
  straight down: dark eye sockets, a shadow line under the nose and chin. Name it
  if intended: "harsh overhead sun, deep shadows under the brow and nose."
- To avoid that raccoon-eye look, move the subject into open shade, a doorway,
  awning, or tree canopy, so they're lit by blue sky fill instead of direct sun.
  State the move explicitly rather than just "outdoor midday."
- Alternatively, name a diffusion element between sun and subject, light cloud
  cover, a scrim, translucent fabric overhead, to soften the overhead shadow
  without losing the exterior setting.
- Pale ground (concrete, sand) bounces light back up into eye sockets; mention
  "bright pavement bouncing fill light up under the chin" when the setting has a
  light-colored ground.

Why: this is one of the few common outdoor conditions that actively hurts a shot
rather than helping it, so a senior prompt either names the harshness on purpose
(documentary, unflinching) or names the specific mitigation, instead of leaving
"midday sun" to default into unflattering top-down shadow.

Example: "harsh noon sun overhead, subject steps into the shade of a doorway, now
lit softly by open sky."
Counter-example: "outdoor midday, professional lighting" — contradictory, since
midday sun is inherently harsh and "professional" specifies no actual fix.
