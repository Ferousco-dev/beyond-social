A working cinematographer crops a frame to hide what a shot doesn't need; use the
same instinct to keep unstable hand geometry out of camera rather than asking the
model to render it perfectly.

Framing choices that reduce visible hand error:

- Shoot from a shoulder-height or over-the-shoulder angle so hands stay at the
  bottom edge of frame, partially cropped by the frame line rather than fully
  visible.
- Use a longer lens and shallower depth of field so a hand in the foreground
  falls outside the focal plane and reads as a soft, secondary shape.
- Favor a medium close-up on the face or product with hands entering only at the
  frame edge for a beat, rather than a wide shot that puts full hands center-frame.
- When a hand must be sharp and central (holding a product label to camera),
  hold it still for the whole shot instead of moving it into that position on camera.
- Silhouette or backlight hand actions that are secondary to the shot's point,
  since silhouette hides finger-count errors entirely.

Why: audiences read a shot's subject from what's largest, sharpest, and most lit
in frame. If hands are none of those things, viewers' attention and the model's
generation budget both go to the part of the frame that actually needs to look
right, and a soft or cropped hand error is far less noticeable than a sharp one.

Example: "over-the-shoulder medium shot, product label in sharp focus, a hand
enters softly at the bottom edge of frame holding the box, out of focus."
Counter-example: "wide static shot, full body, both hands raised and gesturing
at chest height in sharp focus" — puts the least reliable geometry at the exact
center of visual attention with nothing to hide it.
