Unprompted, generated faces often lock eyes at a fixed, slightly unfocused point
that never shifts, which is the classic "dead-eyed" look; naming a specific gaze
target and letting it move gives the eyes something believable to do.

How to direct gaze:

- Name a target, not a direction in the abstract: "looking at the camera lens,"
  "looking down at the phone in their hand," "eyes on the product label as they
  turn it," rather than "looking around" or "engaged expression."
- Let the gaze target shift once or twice within a longer shot (glance to the
  product, back to camera) instead of holding one fixed point for the entire
  duration; a single unbroken lock read as artificial past about two seconds.
- For direct-to-camera delivery, specify the eyes hold the lens, not a point near
  it; a gaze that's slightly off-axis from the lens is one of the fastest tells
  that a talking-avatar shot is synthetic.
- Match gaze to head movement: if the head turns toward an object, the eyes should
  lead the turn by a fraction of a beat, not follow it or ignore it entirely.
- Avoid asking for "thoughtful" or "distant" gazes as standalone descriptors; they
  have no fixed target and the model tends to render them as an empty stare.

Why: human gaze is goal-directed almost all the time, fixed on something specific
even in passing glances, and viewers read a target-less stare as a signal of
non-presence faster than almost any other facial cue.

Example: "eyes on the camera lens for the opening line, then glance down to the
product in hand on 'like this,' back to the lens to close."
Counter-example: "distant, contemplative gaze into the middle distance" — with no
target, the eyes settle into an unfocused, slightly crossed stare across frames.
