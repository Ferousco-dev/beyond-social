Left unguided, generated faces smooth toward an airbrushed, poreless default that
reads instantly as synthetic; naming actual skin texture and a specific, slightly
imperfect light source pulls it back toward a photographed face.

What to name explicitly:

- Texture words a cinematographer or retoucher would use: visible pores, faint
  under-eye shadow, a little sheen on the forehead and nose bridge, not "flawless
  skin" or "glowing skin."
- A single motivated key light with a name: window light from one side, a practical
  lamp in frame, overcast daylight; avoid "soft studio lighting" as a default,
  since it's what pushes the model toward the flat, shadowless plastic look.
- Some light falloff and shadow on one side of the face; a face lit evenly from
  all directions has no key light logic and reads as rendered, not shot.
- Skin tone variation: slightly warmer at the cheeks and nose, cooler at the temples,
  rather than one flat tone across the whole face.
- If shooting handheld or UGC-style, ask for the small exposure and white-balance
  imperfections a phone camera actually produces rather than color-corrected,
  even skin tones.

Why: real skin scatters and absorbs light unevenly because it has texture, oil,
and translucency; a model that isn't told this defaults to a smooth, uniform
material more like painted plastic than skin, and even light with no falloff has
no real-world light source that produces it.

Example: "window light from camera-left, visible skin texture and pores, slight
sheen on the forehead, soft shadow falling across the right side of the face."
Counter-example: "flawless glowing skin under soft studio lighting" — produces the
even, poreless, shadowless look that is the single most recognizable AI-video tell.
