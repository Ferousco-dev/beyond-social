Camera height, eyeline, and approximate focal length should stay consistent
across shots of the same scene, unless a lens change is a deliberate
storytelling beat.

The recipe:

- State camera height relative to the subject in every shot ("eye-level,
  camera at seated height, roughly four feet off the floor").
- Hold one focal-length family per scene (a consistent 35mm-equivalent look,
  say) instead of swinging between an extreme wide and a long lens shot to
  shot.
- Name the resulting depth-of-field character (softly blurred background
  versus sharp) and keep it constant for shots meant to read as continuous.
- Reserve a focal-length or height change for a beat that motivates it — a
  reveal, an emotional escalation — and state that reason in the prompt rather
  than letting a generation pick a lens at random.

Why: real single- or multi-camera coverage of one scene uses a matched lens
kit and a consistent eyeline by convention, because it's how a crew keeps
every angle feeling like it belongs to the same room and the same moment.
Mismatched focal lengths make an edit feel like footage from two different
shoots even when the composition of each shot is otherwise fine.

Example: "eye-level camera, 35mm-equivalent lens, subject occupies the middle
third of frame, background softly out of focus" held across shots 1 through 3.
Counter-example: shot 1 is a wide establishing shot from a low angle, shot 2
cuts to an extreme telephoto close-up with heavily compressed background —
no visual bridge, reads like mismatched stock footage.
