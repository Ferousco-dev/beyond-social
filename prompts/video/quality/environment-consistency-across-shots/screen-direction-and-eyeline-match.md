A subject's gaze direction and the direction they exit or enter frame must
match logically across cuts, or the spatial relationship between subjects and
objects reads as broken even when each shot looks fine in isolation.

The recipe:

- If a subject looks at an off-screen object or person in one shot, keep
  their eyeline angle consistent with where that object or person sits in the
  reciprocal shot.
- If a subject exits frame in one direction, have them enter the next shot
  from the opposite edge (exit screen-right, enter screen-left) to preserve
  continuous travel, unless a cut motivates a full location reset.
- In a two-person dialogue, keep each subject's eyeline angle stable across
  their own coverage — don't let one shot show subject A looking slightly up
  while the reverse shot shows them looking level, implying a height mismatch.
- When a subject looks toward camera-adjacent off-screen space (interview
  style), keep the exact same off-axis angle in every shot of them, implying
  one fixed, real conversation partner.

Why: eyeline and screen direction are how an edit encodes spatial
relationships without ever showing the whole space at once. When they agree,
the audience unconsciously reconstructs a coherent room; when they contradict
each other, the cut reads as spatially wrong even to viewers who can't say
why.

Example: "subject A exits frame screen-right; subject B, next shot, enters
frame from screen-left" — tying two shots into one continuous hallway.
Counter-example: the subject exits screen-right in shot 1 and enters from
screen-right again in shot 2 with no location change stated — implies they
walked in a circle.
