Symptom: the subject sits precisely in the middle of the frame with even
headroom and balanced negative space on both sides, shot after shot. It's
technically tidy and instantly recognizable as generated, because working
camera operators almost never center a subject by default; real framing has
a bias, and the model's un-directed default is dead center.

- Specify a rule-of-thirds placement explicitly: "subject positioned on the
  left third, negative space to the right" — do not leave framing unstated
  and expect an off-center result, since centered is the path of least
  resistance for the model.
- Give the frame a lead: when a subject faces or moves in a direction, put
  the open space in front of them, not behind ("looking off to the right,
  extra space on the right side of frame") the way a camera operator
  naturally leads a moving subject.
- Vary headroom on purpose: a little more or less air above the subject than
  feels "safe" reads as a human framing choice; perfectly even headroom
  across every shot reads as templated.
- Break symmetry in the background too: an off-center window, a prop to one
  side, an asymmetric line in the environment all work against the
  too-tidy feel that centered framing plus a symmetrical set produces together.
- Reserve true centered symmetry for the moments it's earned (a formal
  product hero beat, a deliberate confrontational stare into camera) rather
  than defaulting to it everywhere.

Why: professional composition is directional and asymmetric because it
encodes information (where attention should go, where the subject is headed);
uniform centering carries no such information and is the visual fingerprint
of a shot nobody actually composed.

Example: "medium shot, subject framed on the left third of frame, looking
off to the right, open space ahead of her gaze."
Counter-example: "subject centered in frame, symmetrical composition" as a
blanket default across every shot in a sequence.
