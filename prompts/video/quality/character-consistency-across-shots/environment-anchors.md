Viewers judge "is this the same scene" partly from the environment, not just the
face. A character who looks slightly different shot to shot reads as more
inconsistent when the room around them is also inconsistent, because there is
nothing stable in frame to anchor the eye.

The recipe:

- Name two or three fixed background elements (a specific window, a doorframe, a
  piece of furniture) and repeat those exact names across every shot's prompt for
  that scene.
- Keep camera-to-background relationship logical across the sequence: if the window
  was on the character's left in a wide shot, it should still be on their left (or
  correctly flipped for a reverse angle) in the close-up.
- State time of day and weather once per scene and keep it fixed unless the story
  calls for a jump; do not let "afternoon light" quietly become "golden hour" between
  shot two and shot five.
- Treat the environment description as part of the same locked block as the
  character's identity clause, so it gets pasted verbatim rather than re-improvised.
- When cutting to a genuinely different location, mark it explicitly as a new scene
  in the shot list so the change reads as an edit, not as environmental drift within
  one supposed continuous scene.

Why: continuity errors compound perceptually. A slightly-off face next to a
consistent, recognizable room reads as "the light changed"; a slightly-off face next
to a room that has also subtly changed reads as "this isn't the same footage,"
because the viewer loses every stable reference point at once.

Example: "same kitchen: white subway tile backsplash, black-framed window over the
sink, on camera-left" named identically in each shot of the scene.
Counter-example: a kitchen with a window in shot one and no window (or a differently
placed one) in shot three, with no story reason for the change.
