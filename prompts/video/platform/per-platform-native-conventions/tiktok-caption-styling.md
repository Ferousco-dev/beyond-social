TikTok's dominant caption convention is CapCut-style karaoke text: short word-groups
revealed in sync with speech, not a full sentence sitting static on screen.

- Reveal one to four words at a time, timed to the syllable, not the whole line at
  once.
- Use a single accent color (commonly yellow, or the brand color) on the one word
  carrying the point; keep the rest white or black with a thin stroke.
- Place captions center-weighted in the mid-to-upper frame, never dead center over
  a face for more than a beat.
- Match the in-app look: a rounded, heavy-weight grotesque, not a thin condensed
  or serif face.
- Drop most punctuation; rely on line breaks and emphasis instead of periods and
  commas.

Why: TikTok's audience is fluent in what the app's own editing tools produce.
A caption style lifted from broadcast or corporate video (a static lower-third,
a serif subtitle) reads as "ad" before the content has even registered, which
triggers a scroll-past. The karaoke-reveal look signals "made by a creator,"
lowering resistance and matching the pacing viewers already expect from the app.

Example: "SO—" (yellow) "—I tried this—" (white) "—for 7 days," revealed
word-group by word-group, synced tightly to the voiceover.

Counter-example: a single full-sentence subtitle bar sitting static at the bottom
of the frame for the whole clip, set in a thin serif font. It reads as a
repurposed ad, not something made on the platform, and viewers scroll past it
before finishing the sentence.
