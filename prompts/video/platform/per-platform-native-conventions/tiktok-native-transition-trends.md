TikTok has a rotating vocabulary of native transition techniques, a hand
covering the lens, a snap-zoom, a match-cut through a doorway, that read as
made-on-the-app because they mimic what the in-app editor and its trend cycle
actually produce.

- Use a hand, object, or body part crossing the lens as the wipe trigger (a
  hand covers the camera, pulls away on the next scene) rather than a
  software cross-dissolve.
- Match motion across the cut: swing an object or turn a body in the same
  direction and speed on both sides so the transition reads as one continuous
  gesture, not two takes stitched with an effect.
- Anchor the transition to a specific sound, a whoosh, a snap, a beat hit,
  since TikTok trend transitions are audio-triggered, not purely visual.
- Avoid soft cross-dissolves and cinematic gradient wipes; they read as
  editing-software output, not app-native, and instantly signal a produced ad.

Why: TikTok viewers are extremely fluent in what the app's own creation tools
and trend cycles look like, so a transition that mimics those mechanics,
motion-matched, object-triggered, sound-anchored, reads as authentically made
on the app, while a generic editing-software transition reads as imported
content from elsewhere.

Example: a hand slaps over the lens on a snare hit, pulls away to reveal the
next location, both motions matched in speed and direction.

Counter-example: a one-second cross-dissolve with a subtle color gradient
between two unrelated shots. Technically smooth, but unmistakably an
editing-software default rather than a native TikTok transition.
