Where a testimonial is shot signals whether the moment was staged for the camera or caught inside an ordinary life already in progress, and the second read is the entire point of the format.

Rules for picking and dressing the setting:

- Choose a location tied to the product's actual use (the bathroom counter for skincare, the car for a commute product, the desk for a work tool), not a neutral, unplaceable backdrop.
- Leave one or two unrelated, unstaged objects in frame, a coffee mug, a charging cable, a kid's drawing on the fridge, that the subject clearly didn't arrange for the shot.
- Avoid symmetric, centered product placement in the background; real environments are lived-in and slightly cluttered, not styled.
- Let the location's actual light do the work, whatever window or overhead fixture is really there, instead of adding a flattering key light that a real bathroom or car would not have.
- Keep the frame tight enough that the room reads as specific (a corner of tile, a headrest, a window ledge) rather than wide enough to become an obvious set.

Why: a setting that's too clean or too generic is read, correctly, as chosen for the camera rather than found by it, and that single inference undoes the peer-to-peer trust the whole format depends on before the subject says a word.

Example: bathroom counter testimonial with a toothbrush holder and a slightly fogged mirror edge still in frame, window light only.

Counter-example: a testimonial shot against a plain, evenly lit white wall with nothing else in frame, technically clean but placeless, and placelessness reads as a set.
