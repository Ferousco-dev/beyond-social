The words a testimonial subject says carry as much credibility risk as the footage: a vague superlative reads as scripted no matter how natural the delivery is, while a specific, checkable-sounding detail reads as remembered rather than written.

The rule, applied to every line of testimonial copy:

- Replace "amazing" / "life-changing" / "the best" with a number, a timeframe, or a named comparison: "three weeks," "the fourth one I tried," "cut my routine from twenty minutes to five."
- Name the specific alternative that failed, by category not brand ("the drugstore ones," "a subscription box"), which implies real trial-and-error.
- Attach the claim to a sensory or situational detail unique to this person's use case, not a benefit that could apply to anyone.
- Let one claim stay modest or qualified ("still not perfect, but way better") rather than making every claim maximal; an all-superlative testimonial reads as a review nobody actually left.
- Cut any line that would work equally well in a different product's testimonial; if it's swappable, it isn't specific.

Why: real recollection is granular, people remember the Tuesday they almost returned it, not an abstract five-star feeling, and specificity is exactly the texture that generic ad copy can't fake without sounding like it's trying to.

Example: "I gave it two weeks because that's usually when I quit things. By day nine the flaking was just gone."

Counter-example: "This product is honestly amazing and changed my life, I can't recommend it enough" — three superlatives and zero checkable detail, which is the fingerprint of copy nobody actually lived through.
