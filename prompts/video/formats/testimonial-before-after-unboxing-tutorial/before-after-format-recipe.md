A before/after ad's entire argument is a single visual comparison, and the structure exists to make that comparison land as one clean beat rather than a vague sense of improvement over time.

The beat sheet:

- Establish the before state plainly: no dramatic music, no flattering angle, the actual starting condition shown at a normal, slightly unglamorous baseline.
- Name the timeframe and the effort explicitly, on screen or in voiceover ("day 1" to "day 14," "one use"), so the comparison has a bounded claim instead of an implied one.
- Hold everything except the product's actual effect constant between the two shots: same framing, same light, same expression baseline, same distance from camera.
- Cut directly from before to after on a single hard cut, wipe, or whip pan, no slow dissolve that lets the viewer's eye drift and lose the exact comparison point.
- Hold the after state 1-2 seconds longer than the before state; the after is the payoff and needs the extra beat to register.
- Close on the specific mechanism or product moment that caused the change, not a generic CTA immediately after the reveal.

Why: viewers are primed to distrust before/afters, so the format only works if every non-product variable is visibly controlled; the moment the frame, light, or angle shifts along with the result, the comparison stops looking like evidence and starts looking like a trick.

Example: "Day 1, same bathroom, same overhead light, same neutral expression. [hard cut] Day 14, identical frame, identical light. Two products, that's it."

Counter-example: a soft-focus, warmly lit "after" shot cut against a flat, harshly lit "before" shot with a slow dissolve between them, the lighting change alone does more visible work than the product.
