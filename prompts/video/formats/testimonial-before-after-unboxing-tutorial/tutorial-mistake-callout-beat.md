A brief cutaway showing the common wrong way to do a step, placed right before or after showing the correct technique, raises a tutorial's perceived expertise more than any amount of confident narration, because it proves the presenter has seen the failure mode, not just the success case.

How to place and build the beat:

- Insert exactly one mistake callout per tutorial, positioned at the step where beginners most often go wrong, not evenly spaced or added to every step.
- Show the mistake briefly and concretely (too much pressure, wrong angle, wrong order), not described only in voiceover; the viewer should see the error, not just be told about it.
- Cut directly from the mistake to the correct technique on the same action, same framing, so the contrast is visually immediate rather than separated by other content.
- Keep the callout under 1-2 seconds; it is a contrast beat, not a second tutorial about what not to do.
- Frame the callout with a small verbal or on-screen marker ("not this," a red X, a quick "common mistake") so it reads unambiguously as the wrong example and not as an alternative valid method.

Why: viewers extend more trust to instruction that names specific failure modes than to instruction that only shows success, because naming the mistake is evidence of firsthand experience with the process, which is exactly the signal a tutorial needs to feel earned rather than recited from a script that was never actually tested.

Example: "[correct fold shown] like this, not— [quick cutaway: over-tight fold, on-screen X] too tight and it'll tear. [back to correct technique, continues]"

Counter-example: a tutorial that only narrates "be careful not to overtighten it" without ever showing what overtightening looks like, leaving the actual failure mode, the thing most likely to trip up a first attempt, entirely undemonstrated.
