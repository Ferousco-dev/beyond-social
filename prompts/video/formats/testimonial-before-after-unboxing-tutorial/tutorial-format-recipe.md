A short-form tutorial ad sells competence transfer, the promise that watching this gets the viewer a real result, and its structure has to earn that promise inside a runtime too short for a full explanation.

The beat sheet:

1. Outcome hook (0-2s): show the finished result first, or state it plainly, so the viewer knows exactly what they're about to learn to do.
2. Setup (2-4s): the tools, ingredients, or starting state laid out in one clean shot, named on screen or in voiceover.
3. Numbered steps (bulk of runtime): each step as its own short beat, one action per beat, in strict chronological order, with a number or short label on screen.
4. One mistake callout (brief, mid-tutorial): a quick "don't do this" cutaway showing the common error, contrasted against the correct technique just shown.
5. Result confirmation: the finished state shown again, ideally matching the hook's framing, to close the loop the opening promised.
6. CTA: tied to the outcome just delivered ("save this for next time"), not a generic follow prompt.

Why: a tutorial's runtime is too short to explain everything, so the structure's job is to make the viewer feel they got the whole method in the time given; skipping the outcome hook loses viewers who don't yet know what they'd be learning, and skipping numbered chronological steps forces the viewer to reconstruct the order themselves, which most will not bother doing.

Example: "[finished braid, 1s] here's how in four steps. [tools laid out] 1. section the hair. 2. cross right over. [mistake cutaway: too tight] not this tight. 3. cross left. 4. secure. [finished braid again] save this."

Counter-example: a tutorial that opens with "today we're making X" and narrates the full process as one continuous unbroken take with no step markers or numbering, leaving the viewer to guess where one step ends and the next begins.
