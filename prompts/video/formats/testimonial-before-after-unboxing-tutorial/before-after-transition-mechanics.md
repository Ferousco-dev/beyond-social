The specific cut used at the before-to-after moment shapes how the comparison is read: a hard cut asks the viewer to compare two frozen states, while a whip pan or wipe asks them to feel a single continuous transformation.

The options and when each earns its place:

- Hard cut on a match frame (same crop, same pose) for maximum comparability; this is the default and the safest choice when the visual difference itself needs to be scrutinized, skin, a repaired object, a cleaned surface.
- Whip pan (fast blur pan away and back) timed to a sound hit, used when the product's action itself is the story (a wipe, a swipe, a spray) and the motion sells the mechanism, not just the result.
- Vertical or horizontal wipe transition, used mainly for on-screen "before/after" split reveals where both states briefly coexist side by side before settling on the after.
- Never use a slow cross-dissolve; the blend period lets the viewer's eye lose the exact comparison point and reads as a soft, ambiguous transformation rather than a hard proof.
- Time whichever transition to a beat, a VO word, a snap, or a sound effect, so the cut feels intentional rather than arbitrary.

Why: the transition is the moment the entire claim is either proven or fudged, and each transition type sets a different implicit contract with the viewer about what kind of comparison they're about to judge; picking the wrong one either invites scrutiny the footage can't survive or wastes drama the mechanism deserves.

Example: "whip pan on the spray sound, camera blurs and resettles on the identical frame, now clean."

Counter-example: a two-second cross-dissolve between before and after with soft focus in the middle frame, the viewer never gets a clean instant to compare the two states against each other.
