Unboxing voiceover works best as live sensory reporting of what's happening in the hands right now, not as marketing copy read over unrelated footage; the words should describe what the mic and camera are catching, in the moment it's caught.

The recipe:

- Write in present tense, first person, describing the physical sensation as it happens: "okay, this lid has actual weight to it," not "this product features a premium metal lid."
- Sync specific words to specific frames: the word "click" lands on the frame the clasp releases, "so soft" lands on the frame fingers touch the material, not a beat before or after.
- Include at least one unscripted-sounding reaction to something minor and non-marketed, the box's weight, a smell, a small design detail, which signals the narration is reporting, not reciting a brief.
- Keep sentences short and let them trail off mid-action rather than always completing a thought before the next visual beat; real live narration doesn't wait for the perfect clause break.
- Avoid feature-sheet vocabulary entirely in this narration; save specs and claims for on-screen text or a separate beat, not the sensory voiceover track.

Why: the credibility of unboxing narration comes from sounding like commentary generated by the physical experience itself, and any line that could have been written without ever touching the object, a spec, a benefit claim, a superlative, breaks that illusion the instant it's heard against footage of hands actually holding something.

Example: "oh, that's heavier than I expected— [clasp click] okay that click is so satisfying."

Counter-example: "This premium organizer features a durable aluminum finish and ergonomic design," read in an even, enthusiastic tone over footage of hands opening the box, describing the product like a listing rather than reacting to holding it.
