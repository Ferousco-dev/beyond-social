A testimonial ad can run as one person's full arc or as a fast montage of several different people each saying a fragment, and the choice changes what the format is proving: depth of one story versus breadth of consensus.

How to choose and build each:

- Single-subject: use when the product needs explanation or the trust barrier is high (skincare results, a service, anything with a learning curve); give the full struggle-turn-proof arc and let the pacing breathe with real pauses.
- Multi-customer montage: use when the claim is simple and the goal is social proof by repetition; each person delivers one sentence or fragment, cut hard on the beat, no ramp-up per person.
- In a montage, vary the setting, framing, and camera height between subjects (kitchen, car, gym) so repetition reads as separate real customers, not one script performed by different actors.
- Never blend the two: a montage that suddenly gives one subject a 15-second arc breaks the rhythm the format set up, and a single testimonial interrupted by a stranger's one-liner breaks the intimacy.
- Match the closing beat to the choice: single-subject closes on their own recommendation; montage closes on a rapid-fire stack of the same word or result from each subject.

Why: a montage's persuasive power is volume and variety, it says "many independent people agree," while a single testimonial's power is depth, it says "this specific transformation is real." Mixing the two dilutes both effects into a format that argues neither point clearly.

Example: three different kitchens, three different people, each on screen under two seconds: "changed my mornings." / "wish I bought it sooner." / "worth every cent."

Counter-example: four customers each getting a full 8-second arc back to back, an 32-second video that feels like four short ads stapled together rather than one cohesive piece of proof.
