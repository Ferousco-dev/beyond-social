Unboxing anticipation is a function of held time, not held mystery; the withholding phase needs enough duration for the viewer to actively want the reveal, but not so much that a short-form video's attention budget runs out first.

How to budget it inside a 15-30 second video:

- Spend roughly a third of total runtime on everything before the product is visible: exterior, approach, opening action. Less than that and the reveal has no tension to release; more than that risks losing viewers before the payoff.
- Within the withholding phase, vary shot duration rather than holding one static shot the whole time, a 1s exterior, a 2s approach, a slower 2-3s opening action, so the pacing itself builds rather than stalls.
- Use one micro-pause immediately before the final reveal, a half-second hold on the lifted lid or parted tissue, as the anticipation peak; this is the single most compressible beat if the video is running long, and the single most damaging beat to cut entirely.
- After the reveal, do not return to a slow pace; the back half of the video should move faster than the front half, since the tension has already paid off.
- If the product itself is not visually striking, shorten the withholding phase; a plain object doesn't support a long build and the format will feel padded rather than anticipatory.

Why: anticipation is a felt duration, not an abstract idea of "building suspense," so the actual seconds spent before the reveal are doing measurable work, and both under- and over-spending that budget are equally visible to a viewer even if they couldn't name why the pacing felt off.

Example: "3 shots, 1s / 2s / 2.5s, building to a half-second hold on the lifted lid, then a fast 2s reveal and detail cut."

Counter-example: an 8-second static shot of the closed box before anything happens, followed by a rushed one-second reveal, the budget is inverted from where the tension actually needs to live.
