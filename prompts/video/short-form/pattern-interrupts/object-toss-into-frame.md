Having an object enter the frame unexpectedly, thrown, dropped, swiped, or
kicked into shot from off-screen, redirects attention because peripheral
motion is processed before central, static content is.

- Enter from a frame edge, never spawn or fade in at center; the entry point
  matters as much as the object itself.
- Give the object a real trajectory: a thrown item follows a parabolic arc
  under gravity, a dropped item accelerates, a swiped item decelerates on a
  surface with friction. An object that moves in a straight line at constant
  speed reads as composited or generated rather than physically thrown.
- Let it land with a small settle: a bounce, a wobble, a slight overshoot
  before it's still, since real objects rarely stop the instant they land.
- Sync the impact sound to the actual landing frame, not the moment it
  enters frame.
- Keep the object small relative to the frame on entry so its growth in
  apparent size as it approaches reads as depth, not a jump cut.

Why: motion appearing suddenly at the edge of the visual field is
evolutionarily prioritized processing, the same reflex that makes you notice
something moving in your side vision before you consciously register what it
is. Motivated physics is what keeps the interrupt from reading as a visual
effect: a viewer's motion-prediction system is tuned on a lifetime of real
gravity and friction, and violating it, even slightly, registers as "off"
even to someone who couldn't say why.

Example: "product tossed into frame from the left edge, natural arcing
trajectory, small bounce and wobble on landing, thud synced to the landing
frame."

Counter-example: an object appearing to teleport in and land instantly at
center frame with no arc or settle. The physics reads as unmotivated, which
is one of the fastest tells of synthetic footage.
