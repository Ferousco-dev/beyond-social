Cutting the music bed and ambience down to near-silence for a fraction of a
second immediately before a key claim creates a vacuum the ear rushes to
fill, which pulls attention back onto whatever comes next.

- Drop to room tone, not true digital zero, for roughly 200-400ms.
- Place it directly before the sentence's most important word or the turn in
  the argument, not at a random beat.
- Bring the voice back in at a slightly higher level than before the drop
  (a decibel or two) so the return itself registers as emphasis.
- Use once, at most twice, per video; a silence that recurs on a schedule
  stops reading as a held breath and starts reading as a mixing error.
- Keep a trace of room tone or breath noise under the silence so it never
  reads as a dropped clip or muted stream.

Why: continuous background music and ambience become part of the viewer's
expected baseline within a few seconds, so removing it violates a prediction
the ear has already made, which is a stronger cue than adding a new sound on
top of a busy mix. True digital silence breaks that illusion because it also
removes room tone and breath, which reads as broken audio rather than a
directed pause.

Example: "music and VO drop to room tone for 0.3s directly before 'except
this one thing works differently,' voice returns two decibels louder."

Counter-example: cutting straight to true zero silence for a full second.
Viewers check their volume or assume the clip is broken rather than leaning
in, and the effect reads as a technical fault rather than a deliberate beat.
