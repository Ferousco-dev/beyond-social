A one-to-three-frame overexposed white flash cut between two shots mimics the
visual signature of a real camera flash or a film reel briefly burning out,
producing a luminance spike the eye can't ignore even at a glance.

- Keep the flash to 1-3 frames; longer starts to read as a fade-to-white
  transition rather than a flash.
- Make the flash uneven rather than a flat solid color: a slight flicker or
  a soft vignette that isn't perfectly centered reads as a real light
  overload rather than a digital wipe layer.
- Sync a short tick or click sound to the flash frame so it registers on two
  senses at once.
- Reserve it for a section break, a topic change, or the start of a new
  beat, not for every cut in the video, or it becomes wallpaper.
- Grade the flash slightly warm or slightly cool to match the scene's
  existing light, rather than a neutral white, so it reads as an exposure
  event that belongs to the footage rather than a template asset dropped on
  top of it.

Why: a sudden spike in overall frame luminance triggers the same reflexive
attention response as a real flash of light, distinct from and faster than
a color or content change, because luminance is processed at a lower level
in the visual system than scene content is.

Example: "two-frame white flash with slight uneven flicker, hard cut to the
next scene, soft tick sfx synced to the flash."

Counter-example: a smooth half-second fade to white and back. It's too slow
to register as a flash and instead reads as a generic dissolve, the kind of
default transition that signals templated, AI-assembled editing.
