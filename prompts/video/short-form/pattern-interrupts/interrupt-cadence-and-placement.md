An interrupt placed at a fixed, evenly spaced interval stops being an
interrupt within one or two repetitions, because the pattern of interrupting
becomes a new, learnable pattern in its own right; where and how often an
interrupt fires matters as much as the technique itself.

- Cluster interrupts near the points where a specific piece of content
  historically loses viewers, commonly the first 2-3 seconds and again
  around 7-9 seconds on short-form, and confirm against actual retention
  data rather than assuming those marks apply universally.
- Vary the type of interrupt used within a single video; a whip pan followed
  later by a macro insert followed by a stat callout reads as pacing, three
  whip pans in a row reads as a tic.
- Never repeat the exact same interrupt technique twice in one video.
- Leave 4-8 seconds of pattern-following footage between interrupts so each
  one has a stable baseline to break from; interrupts placed back to back
  cancel each other out because there's no pattern left standing to disrupt.
- Treat the opening 1-2 seconds as a special case: the first frame is doing
  the work a mid-video interrupt does everywhere else, so a video that opens
  strong may not need its first scheduled interrupt until later than usual.

Why: attention and retention curves are built on prediction; a viewer who
has learned that something surprising happens every three seconds simply
starts predicting the surprise, which defeats it. Interrupts work by
violating an expectation, so they need real stretches of unbroken pattern
on either side to have something to violate.

Example: whip pan at 2.5s, macro insert cutaway at 7s, stat callout at 13s,
each a different technique, none of them evenly spaced.

Counter-example: a jump cut inserted every two seconds for the full video.
By the third cut the viewer has learned the rhythm, and the fourth cut lands
as expected rather than as a surprise.
