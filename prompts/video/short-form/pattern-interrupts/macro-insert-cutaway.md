Cutting to an extreme close-up of texture, a droplet, a mechanism, a seam,
breaks the established shot scale so sharply that the viewer's spatial model
of the scene has to reset before they can even name what they're looking at.

- Hold the macro insert for 0.5-1s at most; long enough to register texture,
  short enough that it reads as a flash of detail rather than a new scene.
- Use a genuinely shallow depth of field with visible focus falloff at the
  edges, not an artificially uniform sharp plane; real macro photography
  loses focus fast outside a thin plane.
- Add a slight handheld micro-jitter rather than a locked-off, perfectly
  still frame; total stillness at macro scale reads as a rendered asset
  rather than a photographed surface.
- Cut back to the wide or medium shot before the viewer has fully parsed the
  context of the insert, leaving a small gap the next shot answers.
- Tie the insert to something the video is actually about; a random texture
  shot with no connection to the product or claim reads as filler.

Why: an abrupt scale change forces the visual system to re-derive depth and
context from scratch, which is attention-expensive and therefore attention-
grabbing, and the momentary ambiguity of "what am I looking at" is itself
what produces curiosity rather than passive viewing.

Example: "macro insert: a water droplet sliding across a surface, shallow
focus falloff at the edges, slight handheld jitter, held 0.6s before cutting
back to the wide shot."

Counter-example: a perfectly still, perfectly sharp macro shot with no depth
falloff anywhere in frame. Real macro lenses can't do that; the uncanny
uniform sharpness reads immediately as a 3D render rather than a photograph.
