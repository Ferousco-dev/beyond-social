Introducing a canted horizon line abruptly, on a cut or a hard snap rather
than a slow drift, disrupts one of the most basic spatial anchors a viewer
tracks without thinking about it: that the horizon in frame should be level.

- Tilt somewhere in the 10-20 degree range; smaller reads as accidental
  camera handling rather than a choice.
- Snap into the tilt rather than easing into it, either on a hard cut to an
  already-tilted frame or a fast whip into the angle within a few frames.
- Hold it briefly, under a second, then either correct back to level or cut
  away; a sustained tilt for the rest of the shot stops reading as an
  interrupt and starts reading as a stylistic choice for that whole scene.
- Motivate it with content: a twist in the narration, a problem being
  revealed, a "wait" moment, so the disorientation maps onto something the
  viewer is also feeling about the information.
- If generating the shot rather than filming it, prompt the tilt as a camera
  or gimbal rotation, not a post-crop rotation, so parallax and depth cues
  in the frame stay physically consistent with the angle.

Why: a level horizon is a low-level spatial reference the visual system
maintains automatically, closer to balance and proprioception than to
conscious composition judgment, which is why even a small unexpected tilt
produces a mild disorientation that pulls attention back to the frame.

Example: "hard cut to a 15-degree Dutch angle exactly as the narrator says
'but then everything changed.'"

Counter-example: a subtle 3-degree tilt held for the entire shot. It's too
small to register as intentional and too sustained to function as a snap,
so it just reads as slightly crooked framing.
