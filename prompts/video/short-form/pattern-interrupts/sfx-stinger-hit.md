A single, discrete sound effect, a click, thud, tick, or riser-and-hit, placed
exactly on a cut marks a beat the ear registers independently of whatever
music bed is already running underneath it.

- Choose sounds that plausibly belong to the action on screen (a case
  closing, a tap, a hard footstep) rather than generic library stingers with
  no relation to the image.
- Sync the sound's transient (its sharp attack) to the first frame of the new
  shot, within a single frame of tolerance.
- Vary the stinger type across a video; the same click on every cut trains
  the ear to tune it out within seconds.
- Keep peak level a few decibels under the loudest music moment so the
  stinger reads as an accent, not a competing element.
- Reserve it for cuts that need extra weight; using it on every cut erases
  the distinction it's meant to create.

Why: a sharp attack transient triggers the auditory orienting reflex, the
same low-level mechanism that makes a door slam pull your attention faster
than continuous traffic noise, because sudden onset is a stronger cue than
sustained sound regardless of loudness.

Example: "hard cut synced with a single low thud, transient landing on the
exact first frame of the new shot, no lead-in whoosh."

Counter-example: the same stock "whoosh" placed on every cut throughout the
video. It stops functioning as an accent within the first few uses and
becomes indistinguishable from the bed music the viewer has already tuned out.
