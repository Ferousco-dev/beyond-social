A subject who has been absorbed in a task, in profile or looking away from
lens, suddenly stops and turns to address the camera directly. The switch
from being observed to being spoken to is itself the interrupt, independent
of whatever line follows.

- Motivate the turn with a beat in the action ("wait, actually...", a pause
  mid-chop, setting something down), not an unexplained cut to a new angle.
- Land the eye line exactly on the lens, not slightly off to one side; a
  near-miss eye line reads as looking at a monitor, not at the viewer.
- Cut on the midpoint of the turn itself, catching the motion rather than
  the settled end position, so the movement carries into the new shot.
- Let a small natural pause or breath land before the line, mirroring how
  someone actually gathers themselves before saying something that matters.
- Only works if the video established the subject as not addressing camera
  first; without that baseline there's no pattern to break.

Why: direct gaze toward the viewer activates social attention processing
that indirect gaze doesn't, the same mechanism that makes a stranger's stare
across a room register before you consciously notice it. That only functions
as an interrupt in contrast to an established pattern of the subject not
engaging the camera; it can't be the default state of the whole video.

Example: "subject stops chopping vegetables mid-motion, turns fully to
camera, direct eye contact on lens, brief pause, then: 'okay, but here's the
thing.'"

Counter-example: a video where the subject faces camera the entire runtime.
There's no established pattern of avoidance to break, so nothing about a
later line delivery reads as an interrupt, however direct the eye contact is.
