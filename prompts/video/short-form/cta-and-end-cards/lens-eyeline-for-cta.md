In talking-head short-form, the CTA line is the one moment the subject should
look directly into the lens, even if the rest of the video used an off-axis
or three-quarter eyeline while demonstrating something.

- Shift eyeline to dead-center lens contact only on the CTA sentence, not
  before.
- Keep the head and shoulders roughly still during the shift; a sudden whip
  toward camera reads as a jump cut even when it isn't one.
- Don't add a zoom or push-in at the same moment as the eyeline shift —
  stacking two attention cues at once feels engineered.
- If the rest of the video was handheld with natural micro-movement, keep
  that same handheld quality through the CTA; don't lock the tripod down
  only for the ask.

Why: direct eye contact is the single strongest nonverbal cue for
"this is addressed to you personally," and reserving it for the CTA gives
that one line a different register than the surrounding demonstration
without needing a music sting or a graphic to flag it. Viewers register
the shift subconsciously as a change in address, which is what makes the ask
feel personal instead of appended.

Example: subject demonstrates a technique looking at their hands, then looks
up into the lens for "try this and tell me how it goes."

Counter-example: the subject maintains lens contact for the entire 30-second
video, including the demonstration — there's no differentiation left to mark
the CTA as a distinct moment, so it blends into the rest of the delivery.
