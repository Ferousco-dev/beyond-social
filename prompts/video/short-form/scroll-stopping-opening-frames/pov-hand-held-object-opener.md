Framing the opening shot from the subject's own eyes, hands visible at the
bottom of frame holding or reaching for an object, puts the viewer inside the
body instead of watching it from outside. That embodied framing recruits
attention differently than third-person footage because it reads as
happening to the viewer, not to someone else.

- Camera height and angle match natural eye level or a lowered "looking down
  at my hands" angle, roughly 45-60 degrees down, not a tripod-neutral
  eye-level shot from several feet back.
- Hands enter from the bottom corners of frame, not centered, matching where
  a person's own hands actually sit in their field of view.
- Add slight handheld drift and a touch of natural parallax as the head would
  shift, not a locked-off gimbal move; a too-stable POV shot reads as a rig,
  not a body.
- Keep focal length wide-normal, roughly 24-35mm equivalent, matching how a
  phone or the human eye actually frames a close subject; a long lens POV
  looks like a mounted camera, not a person.

Why: POV framing borrows the visual grammar of first-person experience, which
the viewer's motor and mirror systems process as more immediate than an
observed scene; that immediacy is what makes the frame feel like it's
happening now, to them, which is a stronger scroll-stop than a well-composed
observer shot.

Example: "frame 1: POV looking down, both hands entering from bottom of
frame, fingers just closing around a paper cup, slight handheld sway."
Counter-example: a locked-off, chest-level tripod shot of hands on a table;
technically hands-in-frame, but the fixed, distant camera reads as
surveillance footage, not a body.
