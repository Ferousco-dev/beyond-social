A first frame where an object's apparent size relative to a familiar reference
(a hand, a face, a doorway) is off from what the brain expects reads as
strange before it reads as anything else, and strange stops thumbs. This is
distinct from a close-up; it's specifically about a size relationship the eye
has to double-check.

- Shoot a small object unusually large in frame (a coffee bean filling half
  the screen) or a large object made to look small through forced perspective
  and a long lens compressing the background.
- Anchor the mismatch with one familiar-scale reference partially in frame, a
  fingertip, a corner of a table, so the brain has something to measure
  against and register the discrepancy.
- Keep the rest of the frame's physics consistent, correct shadow direction
  and contact shadow under the object, so it reads as a genuine framing choice
  and not a rendering error.
- Use sparingly per video; scale tricks lose their effect if every frame plays
  the same game.

Why: the visual system uses familiar objects as implicit rulers to judge
scale; when the ruler and the subject disagree, that mismatch triggers an
extra processing pass to resolve it, which is exactly the added dwell time
needed to beat the average scroll speed.

Example: "frame 1: a single blueberry filling most of the frame, a fingertip
just entering from the bottom edge for scale, shallow focus."
Counter-example: an object rendered large with no size reference in frame at
all, ambiguous instead of surprising, and easy to misread as just a blurry
close-up.
