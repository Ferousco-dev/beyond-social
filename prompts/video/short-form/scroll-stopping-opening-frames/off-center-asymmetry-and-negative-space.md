A dead-centered, symmetrically balanced first frame reads as a template or a
title card; it looks composed, and composed reads as an ad before the content
even starts. Pushing the subject off-axis with unequal negative space creates
the visual tension of an unresolved frame, which the eye wants to keep
watching to resolve.

- Place the subject on a rough thirds line, left or right, not centered;
  leave the larger negative-space block on the side the subject is facing or
  moving toward.
- Let something cut into frame at an edge, a shoulder, a hand, a doorframe,
  rather than keeping all edges clean and empty; a fully contained subject
  reads as a posed product shot.
- Vary the negative space asymmetrically: one empty corner and one dense
  corner outperforms four evenly balanced quadrants.
- Reserve true centered symmetry for a deliberate stylistic choice (a mirror,
  a face-on interview format), not as a default.

Why: symmetric, centered framing is what stock photography and template
graphics use because it reads as neutral and safe; neutral and safe is
invisible in a feed built on novelty. Asymmetry creates a visual imbalance the
eye is compelled to scan and resolve, which buys the extra half-second a
scroll-stop needs.

Example: "frame 1: subject on the left third, gaze and body angled right into
open negative space, right edge of frame empty."
Counter-example: subject dead-centered, symmetric background, evenly lit from
both sides, reads instantly as a template intro card.
