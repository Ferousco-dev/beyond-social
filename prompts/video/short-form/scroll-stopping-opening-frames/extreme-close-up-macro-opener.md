Opening tighter than the viewer expects, cropped so tight the subject isn't
immediately identifiable as a whole, creates a small cognitive gap the brain
wants resolved: "what am I looking at?" That gap holds attention through the
first frame in a way a fully legible wide shot does not.

- Fill 80-100% of the frame with texture or detail: skin pores, fabric weave,
  condensation beads, the ridged edge of a bottle cap, shot at true macro
  distance, not just a tight crop of a wide shot.
- Use a shallow depth of field, an 85-105mm-equivalent macro or portrait
  focal length feel, so only a thin plane is sharp and the rest falls into
  soft blur, adding to the ambiguity.
- Pick a detail with implied context, a texture that hints at food, skin, or
  fabric, so the reveal in frame two or three pays off the curiosity rather
  than feeling arbitrary.
- Avoid macro on flat, low-texture surfaces (a plain wall, a screen); with
  nothing to resolve, the ambiguity reads as an error, not a hook.

Why: information gaps trigger curiosity, a well-documented driver of continued
attention. An unidentifiable close-up frame is a visual curiosity gap: the
viewer has to keep watching, at least one more frame, to find out what they're
looking at, and that "one more frame" is the whole battle at the scroll.

Example: "frame 1: macro shot of citrus rind, oil beads visible on the
surface, shallow focus, unidentifiable as a whole fruit yet."
Counter-example: an extreme close-up of a flat gray countertop with no
texture or gradient; nothing to resolve, just looks like a render error.
