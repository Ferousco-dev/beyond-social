An opening frame built from an instantly recognizable scene type, a kitchen,
a car interior, a bathroom mirror, but with exactly one detail that
contradicts what the category predicts, forces a double-take: the brain
categorizes the scene in a glance, then flags the mismatch a beat later.
That two-step recognition holds the eye longer than either a fully normal or
a fully alien frame would.

- Change exactly one variable against the category's default: a bathroom
  mirror shot but the room is outdoors, a coffee cup but it's the size of a
  bucket, a car interior but it's filled with sand.
- Keep every other element of the scene playing it straight and internally
  consistent, correct lighting, correct shadows, correct materials, so the
  single anomaly reads as the point, not as an error alongside other errors.
- Choose an anomaly relevant to the video's actual subject, so the payoff
  in the following frames resolves it, rather than a random gag with no
  follow-through.
- Limit it to one clear deviation; stacking several unrelated anomalies in
  frame one reads as chaotic or broken rather than intriguing.

Why: category recognition is fast and largely automatic; a single violated
expectation inside an otherwise coherent scene creates a specific,
resolvable puzzle rather than generalized confusion, and specific puzzles
are what keep a viewer watching for the explanation instead of scrolling
past a scene they can't parse at all.

Example: "frame 1: a normal bathroom mirror selfie setup, correct lighting
and tile, except the sink is filled with houseplants instead of water."
Counter-example: a scene combining five unrelated surreal elements at once,
floating objects, wrong colors, distorted proportions, reads as broken
rendering rather than an intentional twist.
