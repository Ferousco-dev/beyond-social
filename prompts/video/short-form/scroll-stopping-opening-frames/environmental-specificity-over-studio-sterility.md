An opening frame set against a clean, neutral, uncluttered background reads
instantly as a studio or a render, because real rooms are never that empty.
Populating the background with specific, slightly imperfect, era-and-context-
appropriate detail is what makes a frame register as a real place a viewer
could believe they walked into mid-scroll.

- Include at least two or three incidental background objects that were not
  placed for the shot: a charging cable on a counter, a coffee ring on a
  table, a jacket over a chair back, nothing staged to look tidy.
- Let surfaces show real wear, scuffed paint, a slightly dog-eared book
  spine, a smudge on a mirror, rather than pristine, evenly clean materials.
- Match the light source to a plausible real fixture already in the room
  (a window, an overhead bulb, a lamp) instead of even, shadowless,
  omnidirectional illumination with no visible source.
- Avoid symmetrical, catalog-styled arrangements of objects; real rooms have
  clutter gradients, denser in some corners, sparse in others.

Why: viewers have an enormous trained baseline for what a real room looks
like versus a set or a render, built from a lifetime of seeing both; sterile,
overly tidy, or evenly lit backgrounds trip that trained pattern-recognition
even when the viewer can't articulate why a frame feels off, and that
subconscious "fake" read kills the stop before the content gets a chance.

Example: "frame 1: kitchen counter with a half-full water glass, a folded
dish towel, one drawer slightly ajar, light falling unevenly from a window
camera-left."
Counter-example: an empty, evenly lit white kitchen counter with no objects
and no visible light source, technically clean but reads as a 3D render.
