Split any low-density screen into four quadrants and each carries unequal
attention weight: primary optical area (top-left) gets the first look, strong
fallow area (top-right) gets scanned but not lingered on, weak fallow area
(bottom-left) gets the least attention of the four, and terminal area
(bottom-right) gets the final resting look. This is the Gutenberg diagram.

The recipe:

- Never place a lone, unsupported CTA in the bottom-left quadrant; it's the
  diagram's dead zone.
- Put "must-see" secondary information (price, urgency line) in the top-right;
  it gets scanned even though it isn't lingered on.
- Lean on the terminal area for the primary CTA, since it's where reading
  gravity naturally deposits the eye after edge and diagonal sweeps finish.
- If content forces something into the weak fallow area, add contrast (color
  or size) to counteract the quadrant's natural attention deficit; position
  alone won't save it.

Why: reading gravity comes from the left-to-right, top-to-bottom convention
trained by years of reading text in most Western alphabetic scripts, so the
whole page inherits that directional pull even on a screen with no text to
read at all.

Example: price shown under the product photo in the hero's top-right corner,
"Add to cart" button in the bottom-right.
Counter-example: a secondary "Learn more" link isolated bottom-left with no
supporting icon or color weight. It becomes the least-seen element on the
screen, undermined by position rather than content.
