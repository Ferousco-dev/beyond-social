On-screen hook text in the first one to two seconds of a short-form video has
to sit where the scan pattern's entry point already lands, not wherever
composition looks balanced. The decision to keep watching happens before the
viewer has had a chance to read text placed off the natural scan path.

The recipe:

- Anchor hook text to the upper-third to center-left of frame, matching where
  a compressed 9:16 vertical scan enters, rather than centering it low where
  thumb-controlled UI, captions, the like button, already competes for space.
- Keep the hook to one line, under roughly six words, so it resolves within
  the sub-second window before a swipe decision, the same "first stroke, full
  read" logic as an F-pattern's opening row.
- Don't place hook text over the subject's face; it competes for the same
  fixation point, splitting attention between reading and the pre-attentive
  lock a face otherwise commands.
- Cut or fade the hook text by second two or three once its job, stopping the
  swipe, is done. Leaving it on screen past its useful window adds clutter to
  frames that should be doing other work.

Why: short-form platforms are consumed as a rapid, low-commitment scan across
a feed, structurally closer to scanning a page of low-text hero sections back
to back than to watching one continuous piece of content. The same
entry-point geometry that governs whether a viewer reads a headline governs
whether they read a hook before swiping past it.

Example: "Wait for it..." set top-center-left, one line, gone by second
three, subject positioned lower-right of frame.
Counter-example: hook text centered directly over the speaker's mouth for the
full first five seconds. It fights the face for fixation and overstays the
window where a hook actually does work.
