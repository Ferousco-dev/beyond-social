Stock photography is pattern-matched by visitors within a glance, and once a
page is flagged as "using stock," every other claim on it inherits a discount.

- Shoot real employees or customers in their actual working environment (real
  desk, real product on screen, real clutter), not a seamless studio backdrop.
- Keep natural, asymmetric candid poses and genuine partial expressions rather
  than the direct-gaze, arms-crossed, or laptop-and-coffee poses that define
  the stock-photo genre — those specific poses are what trigger recognition.
- Retain real skin texture and minor asymmetry in retouching; heavy skin
  smoothing and symmetry correction produce the same uncanny "too perfect"
  read as an over-processed AI face.
- Vary framing and background across people on a team page instead of
  running every headshot through an identical studio template — perfect
  uniformity across many photos is itself a stock-library tell.
- If real photography isn't available yet, say so and leave a placeholder
  rather than filling the gap with a generic stock photo; a labeled gap costs
  less trust than a detected fake.

Why: stock photography carries a specific, learned visual signature — certain
poses, certain lighting ratios, certain background blur patterns — that
frequent internet users recognize almost instantly, consciously or not.
Recognizing "this company didn't bother to show us anything real" transfers
suspicion onto every other trust claim on the page.

Example: "photo of the actual support lead at her real desk, mid-laugh,
monitor visible with real product UI in the background."

Counter-example: a polished stock photo of a diverse team laughing around a
laptop on a white background — a widely reused image that a reverse image
search immediately identifies as not the real team.
