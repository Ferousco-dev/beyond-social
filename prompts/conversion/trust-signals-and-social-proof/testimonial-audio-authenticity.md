A testimonial's audio is as diagnostic as its picture: a voiceover-clean track
under a "customer talking in their kitchen" shot is one of the fastest ways to
break the illusion, because the room and the voice stop matching.

- Keep audible room tone matched to the visible space: light echo in a tiled
  kitchen, a little HVAC hum in an office, near-silence in a carpeted living
  room — silence-under-a-visible-room is a mismatch a viewer feels even if
  they can't name it.
- Let proximity to the mic vary slightly as the person naturally moves or
  turns their head, rather than a perfectly leveled, compressed broadcast
  voice with zero variation.
- Preserve natural speech rhythm: real pauses, a breath before a sentence, a
  trailing "you know" or "honestly" — prompt for these explicitly rather than
  a clean, evenly-paced script read.
- Avoid heavy noise-gating and de-essing that scrubs all room character out of
  the voice; a lightly imperfect, present recording reads as a phone or lav
  mic capture, which is what the format is supposed to be.
- Match emotional pacing to the words: a genuine account of frustration-then-
  relief has uneven pacing, not a steady, upbeat announcer cadence throughout.

Why: audio is processed with less conscious scrutiny than video but carries
just as much signal — a voiceover-polished track is recognized (again, often
subconsciously) as a produced ad register, while natural room tone and
uneven pacing are the acoustic signature of an unscripted, in-the-moment
recording, which is the credibility the format is borrowing from UGC.

Example: "lav mic on a customer in their living room, light room echo audible,
natural pauses and a soft laugh mid-sentence, no noise gate."

Counter-example: a pristine, compressed, noise-free voiceover-quality track
laid under a shot of someone talking in a visibly reverberant tiled bathroom
— the ear catches the mismatch even when the eye doesn't.
