A star score only functions as proof when it is checkable and imperfect; a
bare 5.0 average with no visible source reads as unverifiable, not excellent.

- Always show the review count next to the score ("4.8 · 1,240 reviews"); the
  count is what proves scale, the score alone proves nothing.
- Link the rating to its source platform (Google, Trustpilot, G2, the App
  Store listing) so a skeptical visitor can leave and confirm it themselves.
- Treat a perfect 5.0 as a red flag, not a goal: real aggregate scores with
  meaningful volume cluster in the 4.5-4.9 range, because some fraction of any
  real customer base always leaves 3s and 4s.
- Show the distribution (5-star through 1-star bar breakdown) when space
  allows; a visible sliver of 3-star reviews reads as more honest than a
  suspiciously clean bar chart.
- Keep the number current — a rating widget frozen at a stale count for
  months is itself evidence it's decorative, not live.

Why: modern visitors have been trained by e-commerce and app stores to expect
ratings to be checkable and imperfect. A score that can't be traced to a
source, or that is implausibly perfect, gets discounted by the same instinct
that flags a five-star review with no text as fake.

Example: "4.8 out of 5 · 1,240 verified reviews on Trustpilot" linked to the
live Trustpilot profile.

Counter-example: a static "5.0 stars" graphic with no review count and no
outbound link — it can't be verified, so a skeptical visitor treats it as
decoration rather than proof.
