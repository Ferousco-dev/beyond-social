A press bar borrows the reader's trust in the outlet, but only when the
mention is proportionate and traceable; an inflated press bar is easy to
disprove with one search.

- Use the outlet's actual wordmark, not a generic "As Seen In" badge with
  logos re-drawn from memory — a slightly-off logo is itself a giveaway.
- Link every mention to the live article; an unlinked press logo next to a
  claim invites the exact question a link would have pre-empted.
- Size prominence to actual authority and depth of coverage: a full feature in
  a major outlet earns a larger slot than a one-line mention in a roundup —
  giving them equal visual weight overstates the smaller one.
- Retire mentions that are old enough to be irrelevant to the product's
  current form, or date-stamp them, so the bar doesn't imply current coverage
  that no longer applies.
- Skip the press bar entirely rather than include a single weak or
  years-stale mention dressed up as major coverage; a nearly-empty bar draws
  more attention to its own thinness than no bar at all.

Why: a press mention works as proof by transferring the reader's existing
trust in the publication onto the product, but that transfer only holds up
under a click-through. The moment a claimed mention can't be found or turns
out to be a passing name-check dressed as a "feature," the reader discounts
not just that mention but the surrounding page.

Example: "'As featured in' with the actual TechCrunch wordmark, linked
directly to the published article."

Counter-example: five press logos with no links and one of them from a
five-year-old blog post about an unrelated launch — unverifiable and, if
checked, actively misleading.
