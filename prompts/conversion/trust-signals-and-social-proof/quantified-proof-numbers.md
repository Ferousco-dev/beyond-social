An unrounded number reads as measured; a round number reads as guessed. "14,382
teams" earns more trust than "10,000+ teams" even though the rounded version is
almost certainly the larger real figure.

- Use the actual figure, not a rounded-down marketing ceiling: "3,214
  reviews," not "3,000+ reviews."
- Date-stamp any number that changes over time ("as of Aug 2026") so it
  doesn't read as a stale, copy-pasted claim from a launch page nobody updated.
- Pair the number with its denominator when the claim is a ratio ("92% of 610
  surveyed customers," not a bare "92%"), because an unscoped percentage
  invites the question "out of how many?"
- Prefer a specific, narrow claim over a broad one: "cut onboarding time from
  9 days to 2" beats "dramatically faster onboarding."
- Refresh the number on a real cadence; an unmoving counter next to a "live"
  label is itself a trust break.

Why: this is the precision effect — specificity signals that a number came
from a system of record rather than a marketer's estimate, because rounding is
what people do when they don't actually know the exact value. A visitor
doesn't consciously audit the math, but the jaggedness of a precise figure
registers as "someone pulled this from a database."

Example: "Trusted by 14,382 teams as of August 2026" beside a logo row.

Counter-example: "Trusted by 10,000+ teams worldwide!" with an exclamation
point and no date — the round number and the enthusiasm both read as
marketing copy rather than a fact.
