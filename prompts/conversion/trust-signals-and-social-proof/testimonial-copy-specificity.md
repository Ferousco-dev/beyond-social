A testimonial that only praises is weaker than one that reports a specific,
checkable outcome; "amazing product" is unfalsifiable and reads as filler,
while a number or a named workflow reads as a fact someone experienced.

- Lead with a concrete before/after, not an adjective: "cut our reporting
  time from 6 hours to 40 minutes," not "saves us so much time."
- Include the customer's role and enough context to make the outcome relevant
  to the reader's own situation ("Head of Ops at a 40-person logistics firm"),
  not just a first name.
- Keep one specific, minor imperfection or caveat when the real quote has one
  ("took a week to get the team trained, but now it's automatic") — a
  frictionless, all-upside quote reads as edited.
- Cut generic praise adjectives (amazing, game-changing, incredible) out of
  the pull-quote even if the customer said them; keep the sentence that
  contains the actual result and let that carry the tone.
- Attribute with a full name, role, and company when the customer allows it;
  "Sarah T." with no company is weaker than "Sarah Tran, Ops Lead at
  Fieldwire" because the latter is a person a reader could, in principle,
  find.

Why: unfalsifiable praise triggers the same skepticism as an ad, because
nothing in it could be checked or disproven. A specific number or named
process, on the other hand, is the kind of detail a customer would only know
if the interaction actually happened, which is what makes it register as
testimony rather than marketing.

Example: "We went from 3 days to same-day approval on every application." —
Maria Chen, Underwriting Lead, Northstar Credit.

Counter-example: "This product is a game changer, I love it so much!" —
Maria C. — no outcome, no context, and a truncated name that can't be verified.
