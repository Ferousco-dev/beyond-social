A logo bar earns trust through recognizability, not through count; six
instantly-known brands outperform twenty unfamiliar ones.

- Lead with the single most recognizable logo in the leftmost or first
  position; scanning is left-to-right and front-loaded recognition anchors the
  whole row as credible before the eye reaches the rest.
- Normalize by optical weight, not bounding-box size: a wordmark and a dense
  icon-plus-text logo at the same pixel height look mismatched, so scale each
  until their visual "ink" reads as equal.
- Render all logos in a single tone (monochrome gray or the brand's own
  neutral) so no one logo's color pulls the eye and breaks the row's rhythm.
- Cap the row at 5-7 logos; beyond that, paginate or rotate rather than
  shrinking type to fit more in, which makes every logo unreadable.
- Only use a logo with permission or a public case study backing it — an
  unverifiable logo is a liability, not proof, if challenged.

Why: a logo bar functions as a fast trust heuristic — the viewer isn't reading
each name, they're pattern-matching "companies I recognize use this," which
happens in under a second. Recognizability, uniform visual weight, and a tight
count are what make that half-second glance register as credible instead of
as clutter.

Example: "grayscale logo row, 6 recognizable companies, uniform optical
height, most-recognizable logo first."

Counter-example: fourteen mixed-color logos of varying size and unknown
companies crammed into one row — it reads as padding, not proof, and forces
the eye to work to find anything familiar.
