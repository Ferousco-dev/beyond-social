A before/after only proves anything if the "before" and "after" are provably
the same conditions except for the product; anything that changes between the
two shots becomes the explanation skeptics reach for.

- Match camera position, focal length, and framing distance between the two
  shots within a few degrees and a few inches; a locked tripod mark on the
  floor is standard practice for a reason.
- Match light direction and time of day, or shoot both in the same
  continuous session; a warmer "after" and cooler "before" reads as a color
  grade doing the work, not the product.
- Keep the background and any visible props identical; a decluttered "after"
  background is a second variable pretending to be the product's effect.
- Let small imperfections carry over both shots (same wrinkle in the shirt,
  same room mess) — the parts that don't change are what make the part that
  did change believable.
- Avoid a hard wipe-transition graphic between two studio-lit grid panels;
  a single continuous shot with a visible transition (a pan, a door, a time
  jump) reads as one real event rather than two staged photos stitched together.

Why: viewers are primed to distrust before/afters because the format is
famous for cheating through lighting, angle, and selective framing. Holding
every variable constant except the product removes the alternative
explanations, which is the actual mechanism of persuasion here, not the
transformation itself.

Example: "same tripod mark, same window light at 4pm, same background,
continuous pan from before to after across a single take."

Counter-example: a moodily lit, color-graded "before" cut against a bright,
wide-angle "after" shot from a different angle — the lighting and lens change
alone would explain the difference, so the comparison proves nothing.
