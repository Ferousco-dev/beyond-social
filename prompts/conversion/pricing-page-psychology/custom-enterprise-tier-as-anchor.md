An unpriced "Contact us" tier sitting at the top of a pricing table does two
jobs at once: it captures enterprise leads who need custom terms, and its mere
presence, priced or not, pulls the perceived value of the tiers below it
upward.

- Place the enterprise tier rightmost or topmost, in the position buyers
  associate with "more," even without a number attached; position alone
  carries the anchoring signal.
- List 2-3 concrete enterprise-only capabilities (SSO, dedicated support, a
  named SLA number) rather than the word "Enterprise" alone; a bare label
  anchors weakly, a specific capability list anchors on real, defensible value.
- Route the CTA to a short, low-friction form (company size, use case) rather
  than a generic "Contact sales" with no context, since the anchor only pays
  off if the lead actually gets captured.
- Do not omit a price entirely with no signal at all; give a floor ("starting
  at $2,000/mo") when the buyer segment skews toward self-serve research,
  since a fully opaque price filters out buyers who would have anchored on it
  positively.

Why: even an unpriced option changes the comparison set. Buyers infer that a
tier requiring a sales conversation costs meaningfully more than the listed
tiers, and that inference alone makes the highest listed price look more
moderate by comparison, the same asymmetric-dominance logic as a decoy tier,
applied to a tier with no stated number at all.

Example: "Enterprise — Custom SSO, dedicated CSM, 99.9% SLA — Talk to sales,"
positioned to the right of a $99/mo Pro plan, making Pro read as the
reasonable, self-serve choice.
Counter-example: a fourth column labeled only "Enterprise" with no features and
a generic "Contact us" link, which anchors weakly and captures no lead context
for the sales team to act on.
