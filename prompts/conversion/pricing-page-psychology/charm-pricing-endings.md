The last digit of a price changes how it is read, because people process
numbers left to right and truncate before they finish reading. A $49 price
outperforms a $50 price by more than the one dollar would predict.

- Use 9-endings ($29, $49, $99) when the brand is positioned on value or
  low friction; the left-digit drop (from 5 to 4, from 10 to 9) registers as a
  meaningfully lower price even though the gap is a cent or a dollar.
- Use round numbers ($30, $50, $100) when the brand is positioned on quality,
  trust, or simplicity; round numbers process fluently and read as confident,
  not haggled-down.
- Never mix conventions across tiers on the same page ($29, $50, $99). The
  inconsistency reads as arbitrary and makes buyers scrutinize the pricing
  logic instead of trusting it.
- Match the ending to the buying context: 9-endings work better for
  low-involvement, fast decisions; round numbers work better for
  high-consideration, high-price purchases where precision reads as care.

Why: this comes from left-digit anchoring research (Thomas and Morwitz, among
others) showing the magnitude judgment happens on the leftmost digit before the
rest of the number is even processed. A 9-ending exploits that partial
processing; a round number instead signals the seller was confident enough not
to round down, which functions as a quality cue in categories where trust
matters more than price sensitivity.

Example: a budget scheduling tool priced at $9/mo, $19/mo, $49/mo across all
tiers. Consistent charm pricing signals value throughout.
Counter-example: an enterprise security product priced at $99/mo mixed with a
$500/mo enterprise tier. The 9-ending on the flagship-adjacent plan undercuts
the trust signal the higher tier is trying to establish.
