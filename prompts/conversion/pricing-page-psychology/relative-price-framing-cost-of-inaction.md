A price in isolation has no meaning; buyers judge it against whatever
comparison is nearest, so placing the right comparison next to the price does
more work than adjusting the price itself.

- Compare against a cost the buyer already accepts and pays regularly (a
  coffee subscription, a single freelance hour, the cost of the problem the
  product solves), stated as a specific number, not a vague gesture at "less
  than you'd think."
- Prefer cost-of-inaction framing over cost-of-alternative framing when the
  product prevents a loss (security, compliance, churn): state what the
  status quo is already costing, in the buyer's own units, not the vendor's.
- Keep the comparison believable and proportionate. A comparison that requires
  the buyer to accept a stretched analogy ("less than your Netflix
  subscription" next to a $400/mo B2B tool) draws attention to the mismatch
  instead of softening the price.
- Use the comparison once, near the price, not as a repeated motif down the
  page; repetition turns a persuasive frame into a tell that the seller knows
  the raw number is a hard sell.

Why: this is anchoring again, but the anchor is a familiar, low-stakes cost
rather than a competitor price, which works because comparing a new expense to
an already-accepted one requires less deliberation than judging it against an
abstract budget. Cost-of-inaction framing works through loss aversion
specifically: a stated ongoing cost of the status quo is processed as a loss
already happening, which people work harder to avoid than they work to obtain
an equivalent gain.

Example: "$29/mo, less than the cost of one missed deadline this quarter,"
paired with a stat on the actual cost of the problem the product addresses.
Counter-example: "$29/mo, less than a fancy coffee" on a page selling
infrastructure monitoring to engineering managers, a comparison so far from the
buyer's actual budget context that it reads as flippant.
