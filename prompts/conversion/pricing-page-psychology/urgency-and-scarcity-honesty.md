Scarcity and urgency move conversion only as long as they are true; a
countdown timer that resets on refresh or a "3 spots left" counter that never
changes gets noticed by a meaningful share of visitors, and once noticed, it
discredits every other claim on the page, not just that one.

- Use urgency only when it is mechanically real: a cohort-based cost or a
  genuine price increase on a fixed date, a seat cap enforced by the backend,
  a launch discount with a real expiry logged server-side.
- Tie the countdown or counter to actual state, not a session-local timer;
  a technical visitor can and will refresh the page to check, and finding it
  reset is worse for trust than never having claimed urgency at all.
- Prefer specific, verifiable framing ("Price increases to $59 on March 1")
  over vague pressure ("Offer ends soon!"); a specific claim is falsifiable and
  therefore more credible, a vague one signals it was written to create
  pressure without a real constraint behind it.
- If there is no genuine scarcity, do not manufacture any. A page with an
  honest, non-urgent price outperforms a page with detected-fake urgency,
  because the fake version taxes trust on every subsequent claim, including
  the ones that were true.

Why: urgency works through loss aversion and a fear of missing a better deal,
but the mechanism depends entirely on the buyer believing the constraint is
real. Once a single scarcity claim is caught as fabricated, buyers apply that
skepticism retroactively to testimonials, feature claims, and guarantees on
the same page, a spillover effect that costs far more than the short-term lift
fake urgency produced.

Example: "Founding member pricing ends March 1 — price increases to $59/mo
after," with the date enforced server-side and shown consistently on repeat
visits.
Counter-example: a "Only 2 spots left!" counter next to an unlimited SaaS plan
with no actual capacity constraint, identical on every page load regardless of
signups.
