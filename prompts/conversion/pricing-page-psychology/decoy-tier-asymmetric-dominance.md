A three-tier layout where the middle plan is strictly better than one of the
other two, on every dimension, makes the middle plan look like the obvious
choice, even to buyers who came in undecided.

- Build the decoy so it is dominated: same or higher price than the target
  plan, but fewer features, on at least the two attributes buyers scan first
  (price and headline feature count).
- Put the decoy adjacent to the target plan in reading order (left-to-right or
  top-to-bottom), so the comparison happens without the buyer scrolling.
- Give the decoy a real audience it is not secretly worthless. If literally
  nobody should buy it, sophisticated buyers notice and the page starts to feel
  engineered against them.
- Limit this to three or four tiers. Adding a second decoy to push two
  different targets splits attention and weakens both comparisons.

Why: the effect (documented by Huber, Payne, and Puto and popularized in Dan
Ariely's Economist subscription experiment) works because relative comparison
is cognitively easier than absolute evaluation. When one option is strictly
dominated by another, the dominant option's value becomes obvious without the
buyer having to reason about what they actually need, and choice share shifts
toward it predictably.

Example: Starter $19 (5 projects), Pro $49 (unlimited projects, priority
support), Team $49 (unlimited projects, no priority support) — Team is the
decoy, Pro is the target, and Pro's extra benefit at the identical price makes
it the visibly better deal.
Counter-example: a decoy priced far below the target with completely different
features (a $9 plan with 1 project next to a $49 unlimited plan). There is no
direct dominance to compare, so the decoy just looks like a separate, valid
option instead of steering the choice.
