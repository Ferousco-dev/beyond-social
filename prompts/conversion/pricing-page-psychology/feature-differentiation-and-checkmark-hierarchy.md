A feature list that repeats the same checkmarks down every tier gives the buyer
no reason to pay more; each tier's list should read as a distinct story about
who it is for, not a superset of ticks.

- List each tier's 3-5 headline features first, in the buyer's language
  ("Unlimited exports"), not the engineering name ("batch_export_v2"), before
  any shared baseline features appear.
- Show what upgrading unlocks by bolding or color-marking only the features
  that are new at that tier, so a buyer scanning left to right sees the
  incremental value instead of re-reading a wall of identical checks.
- Cap the visible list at 6-8 items per tier. Beyond that, buyers stop reading
  line by line and instead pattern-match on list length, which favors the tier
  that merely looks the longest rather than the one that fits.
- Use a dash or muted gray, never a red X, for features a lower tier lacks. An
  X reads as a rejection and adds friction to a page whose job is to move
  people toward a purchase, not to make them feel declined.

Why: buyers compare tiers relatively, and an undifferentiated list forces them
to do the differentiation work themselves by cross-referencing every row,
which most will not do. Front-loading the distinguishing features turns the
list into an argument for upgrading rather than a reference table, and it also
protects the lower tiers from looking punished rather than positioned.

Example: Pro tier lists "Custom domains, Priority support, Team roles" above
the fold of the card, with shared baseline items ("Unlimited projects,
Email support") collapsed below a "Everything in Starter, plus" divider.
Counter-example: a comparison table with 40 identical rows across three
columns, differing only in three scattered checkmarks the buyer has to hunt
for.
