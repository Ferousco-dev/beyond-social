A time-boxed free trial and an always-free tier solve different objections, and
picking the wrong one for the product's time-to-value mismatches the pricing
page's promise with what actually happens after signup.

- Use a free trial (7-30 days, card required) when the product's value is
  clear within the trial window and the buyer needs to see real results before
  committing; state the exact length and what happens at day one after
  expiry ("auto-converts to Pro" or "downgrades to view-only") on the pricing
  page itself, not buried in a follow-up email.
- Use freemium (no card, capped forever) when the product has network effects
  or slow time-to-value, and the free tier should be genuinely useful, not a
  crippled demo, or it fails to build the habit that later converts.
- If requiring a card for a trial, say so next to the CTA in the same visual
  weight as the price ("Free for 14 days, card required"), not in a footnote;
  a hidden card requirement is the single most common trust break on trial
  CTAs.
- Never combine both badly: a "free trial" that is actually a permanently
  capped freemium tier with a different name erodes trust in every future claim
  the page makes.

Why: risk reversal works by shifting the cost of a bad decision from the buyer
to the seller, which lowers the perceived cost of trying. A trial reverses
short-term risk (you can back out before paying); freemium reverses long-term
risk (you never have to pay to keep using it). Mismatching the mechanism to the
product's actual value timeline creates a moment, at day one or at the freemium
cap, where the buyer feels misled about what they agreed to.

Example: "Start your free 14-day trial — no card required" for a tool with fast
time-to-value, converting automatically to view-only (not deleted data) after
expiry.
Counter-example: a "free forever" badge on a tier that silently starts charging
after 30 days, discovered only when the card on file gets billed.
