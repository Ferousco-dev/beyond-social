Labeling one plan "Most Popular" (or "Recommended") does double duty: it is
social proof, and it functions as a default, because undecided buyers follow
the path of least resistance and take the option marked as the norm.

- Put the badge on the plan you actually want most buyers to choose, and make
  sure it is a defensible claim, real usage data if you have it, not a
  marketing invention.
- Reserve the label for exactly one plan. Two "Popular" badges cancel the
  signal, because it stops meaning "most" and starts meaning "we're not sure."
- Combine the badge with a visual default: pre-select its toggle state if the
  page has one, or give its CTA button the highest-contrast fill while
  competitor CTAs stay outlined or ghost.
- Do not badge the cheapest plan by default. That badge amplifies price
  sensitivity and pulls buyers toward the tier with the lowest revenue per
  account, which optimizes the wrong metric.

Why: the label works as social proof (a form of informational conformity,
people infer correctness from what others are doing) and as a status-quo
default at once, and stacking two biases on one element is more effective than
either alone. It also reduces decision fatigue: a buyer facing three
similar-looking plans experiences real cognitive cost in choosing, and a
credible default removes that cost by giving them permission to stop
comparing.

Example: a highlighted "Growth" card with a "Most chosen by teams like yours"
badge, elevated card, and a solid-fill CTA while Starter and Enterprise use
outline buttons.
Counter-example: badging the top-tier "Enterprise" plan as "Most Popular" when
it is priced far above the median customer's budget. The claim is not
believable, and an unbelievable social-proof claim reads as dishonest rather
than persuasive.
